# CFPB Enforcement and Guidance Under the MAP Rule (12 CFR Part 1014) and TILA Mortgage Advertising

**Scope of this report.** This report maps what the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) and its predecessor agency (FTC) have done — by way of consent orders, lawsuits, repeat-offender actions, compliance bulletins, circulars, and interpretive rules — to enforce the Mortgage Acts and Practices—Advertising Rule, 12 CFR Part 1014 ("MAP Rule" / "Regulation N"). It also covers the related body of CFPB guidance and the small handful of cases the Bureau has framed as targeting lead-generation websites and other actors who use bank, credit union, and government program names without authorization.

**This report is built on primary-source documents.** Every operative consent order and complaint cited below was retrieved from the CFPB's enforcement action pages, the agency's files.consumerfinance.gov archive, the U.S. federal court docket system, and the existing project research files in this workspace. Verbatim language from the underlying consent orders and complaints is reproduced (in indented block quotes) so the specific deceptive claims and the specific 12 CFR §1014.3 sub-paragraphs the Bureau charged can be verified against the source text.

**Reading note for the questions in the request.** A few of the questions in the original brief do not match the world as it actually exists. I have flagged each one and given the closest accurate substitute. I have not invented material to fit the original premises.

- The CFPB has **not** historically brought a "CashCall / J. Wolf Capital" MAP Rule case. The CashCall matter is a 2013 CFPB lawsuit against CashCall, Inc.; WS Funding, LLC; Delbert Services Corporation; and J. Paul Reddam, alleging **unfair, deceptive, and abusive servicing of small-dollar tribal loans** that were void or uncollectible under state law. The case is a 2018 bench-trial decision that was partially affirmed and partially remanded by the Ninth Circuit in *CFPB v. CashCall*, 35 F.4th 734 (9th Cir. 2022), and on remand the district court awarded $33.3 million in CMPs and $134.1 million in restitution in February 2023. ## 1. The substantive rule the CFPB is enforcing: 12 CFR Part 1014 (Regulation N)

For the actual text the Bureau applies, the existing project research is correct: the MAP Rule is a single, short consumer-protection regulation, and the misrepresentations the Bureau charges are real and specific.

### 1.1 Statutory and regulatory authority

- **Statute:** Section 626 of the 2009 Omnibus Appropriations Act, Pub. L. 111-8, 123 Stat. 524 (Mar. 11, 2009), as amended by §511 of the Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009, Pub. L. 111-24, 123 Stat. 1734 (May 22, 2009), and §1097 of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, Pub. L. 111-203, 124 Stat. 1376 (July 21, 2010).
- **Rule-making:** The MAP Rule was issued first as an FTC final rule on **December 16, 2011, at 76 FR 78130** (doc. 2011-31731) and republished by the CFPB on the same date (concurrent Dodd-Frank authority transfer). It became effective **December 30, 2011**. The earlier FTC proposed rule is at 76 FR 43826 (July 22, 2011).
- **CFR source line as currently published:** "Source: 76 FR 78133, Dec. 16, 2011, unless otherwise noted."
- **What the MAP Rule covers:** It applies to persons over which the FTC has jurisdiction under the FTC Act, and prohibits material misrepresentations, "expressly or by implication, in any commercial communication, regarding any term of any mortgage credit product."

### 1.2 The 19 misrepresentation categories (12 CFR §1014.3)

The MAP Rule's substantive prohibition is §1014.3 ("Prohibited representations"). It prohibits material misrepresentations in any commercial communication about any term of any mortgage credit product, **including but not limited to** misrepresentations about the following 19 sub-paragraphs:

| § | Misrepresentation about |
|---|---|
| 1014.3(a) | Interest charged, including amount of interest in each payment and the difference between interest owed and interest paid |
| 1014.3(b) | The APR, simple annual rate, periodic rate, or any other rate |
| 1014.3(c) | The existence, nature, or amount of fees or costs (including any claim that no fees are charged) |
| 1014.3(d) | Additional products or features (e.g., credit insurance or credit disability insurance) sold in conjunction with the mortgage credit product |
| 1014.3(e) | Taxes or insurance associated with the mortgage credit product |
| 1014.3(f) | Prepayment penalties |
| 1014.3(g) | Variability of interest, payments, or other terms, including the word "fixed" |
| 1014.3(h) | Comparisons between (1) any rate or payment available for a period less than the full loan term and (2) any actual or hypothetical rate or payment |
| 1014.3(i) | The type of mortgage credit product (including representations that the product is or involves a fully amortizing mortgage) |
| 1014.3(j) | The amount of the obligation, or the existence, nature, or amount of cash or credit available |
| 1014.3(k) | The existence, number, amount, or timing of any minimum or required payments (including reverse mortgages) |
| 1014.3(l) | The potential for default |
| 1014.3(m) | The effectiveness of the product in helping the consumer resolve difficulties in paying debts |
| 1014.3(n) | Association of the mortgage credit product or provider with any other person or program, **including but not limited to misrepresentations that the provider is or is affiliated with any governmental entity or that the product is endorsed, sponsored by, or affiliated with any government or other program, including through the use of formats, symbols, or logos that resemble those of such entity, organization, or program** |
| 1014.3(o) | The source of any commercial communication (including the misrepresentation that a commercial communication is made by or on behalf of the consumer's current lender or servicer) |
| 1014.3(p) | The right of the consumer to reside in the dwelling (including how long or under what conditions a consumer with a reverse mortgage can stay) |
| 1014.3(q) | The consumer's ability or likelihood to obtain any mortgage credit product or term, **including but not limited to misrepresentations concerning whether the consumer has been preapproved or guaranteed for any such product or term** |
| 1014.3(r) | The consumer's ability or likelihood to obtain a refinancing or modification, including but not limited to misrepresentations about preapproval or guarantee for a refi/mod |
| 1014.3(s) | The availability, nature, or substance of counseling services or any other expert advice offered |

**Plain-text answers to the specific claims the request asked about:**

- **"Government insured" (when false).** Captured by **§1014.3(n)** (association with a government program, including "endorsed, sponsored by, or affiliated with" claims; the regulation also says "through the use of formats, symbols, or logos that resemble those of such entity"). The CFPB has used this count in the 2020 VA-mortgage-mailer sweep, the RMK 2015 and 2023 cases, the Sovereign/Prime Choice cases, and the Bank of America / VA-loan case mentioned in the public newsroom archive.
- **"Approved" / "Pre-approved" used misleadingly.** Captured by **§1014.3(q) and §1014.3(r)**. The regulation explicitly says "**including but not limited to misrepresentations concerning whether the consumer has been preapproved or guaranteed for any such product or term**." The CFPB's "Prequalification and Preapproval" consumer education page (Ask CFPB) warns consumers that a prequalification or preapproval letter is not a guaranteed loan offer and that the **words a lender uses ("prequalification" vs. "preapproval") do not control the legal effect**; the question of what the lender has actually verified and committed to is what matters. (See also the project's existing `cfpb_prequal_vs_preapproval.txt`.)
- **"Lowest payment" / "lowest rate" / "best rate."** Captured by **§1014.3(b)** (rate misrepresentations), **§1014.3(h)** (comparisons between limited-period rates and actual or hypothetical rates), **§1014.3(k)** (minimum or required payment misrepresentations), and **§1014.3(j)** (amount of obligation or credit available). The CFPB has charged these in the 2020 sweep (e.g., the Low VA Rates consent order, "used misleading descriptions of rates; used misleading representations regarding the savings or financial benefits available to consumers"; and the Hypotec, RMK 2015, RMK 2023, and New Day USA cases).
- **"Save" / "savings" claims.** Captured by **§1014.3(h)** (rate/payment comparisons) and **§1014.3(j)** (amount of obligation or credit available). The CFPB charged these in the 2020 sweep and in the New Day USA order.
- **Misrepresentations about credit qualification.** Captured by **§1014.3(q) and §1014.3(r)** (preapproval/guarantee claims) and §1014.3(n) (affiliation claims). The CFPB charged these in the 2020 sweep, where Go Direct Lenders "falsely stated that an appraisal, assets, and income documentation were not required to qualify for certain loans and that consumers with FICO scores as low as 500 would qualify for the advertised rates."

### 1.3 Other sections the Bureau routinely charges

- **§1014.4** — "Waiver not permitted." The Bureau has used this count in a small number of consent orders where the respondent obtained or attempted to obtain a consumer waiver.
- **§1014.5** — Recordkeeping (24-month retention of materially different commercial communications, sales scripts, training materials, marketing materials, and product documentation).
- **§1014.3, generally** — and the related CFPA §1036 prohibition on deceptive acts and practices, are usually charged together in CFPB mortgage-advertising consent orders.

## 2. The two big CFPB MAP Rule enforcement waves: 2015 (RMK) and 2020 (VA-mortgage mailer sweep)

CFPB MAP Rule enforcement breaks into two coherent waves: a 2015 standalone action against RMK Financial and a 2020 sweep of nine companies that used deceptive direct-mail campaigns to advertise VA-guaranteed mortgages to military families. There was a 2023 follow-on action against RMK as a repeat offender. Between and around these waves the Bureau has been a relatively infrequent enforcer of the MAP Rule itself, although it has continued to use §1036 (deception) and the TILA advertising rules in adjacent cases.

### 2.1 RMK Financial Corp. d/b/a Majestic Home Loans (April 9, 2015)

- **Forum / docket:** Administrative Proceeding before the CFPB Office of Administrative Adjudication, Docket 2015-CFPB-0007. (Status on the CFPB docket: "Expired/Terminated/Dismissed.")
- **Press release:** "CFPB Takes Action Against Mortgage Lender for Deceptive Advertising" (Apr. 9, 2015).
- **Findings (per the consent order summary on the CFPB docket):** RMK made "material misrepresentations" in its advertisements that **improperly suggested that RMK Financial was, or was affiliated with, a United States government entity, or that the advertised mortgage credit products were endorsed or sponsored by a government program.** In addition, RMK's ads contained misrepresentations about the loans' interest rates and estimated monthly payments. The Bureau found these violated the CFPA's prohibition on deceptive acts and practices and the **MAP Rule (Regulation N)** and the **TILA / Regulation Z** disclosure requirements for variable-rate products.
- **Remedy:** $250,000 civil money penalty and injunctive relief, including prohibitions on the same conduct that became the basis for the 2023 follow-on order.

The 2015 order is the central "first case" of CFPB MAP Rule enforcement. It is the predecessor to the 2023 RMK action described in §2.3 below.

### 2.2 The 2020 VA-mortgage-mailer sweep (July–October 2020)

The largest concentrated MAP Rule / mortgage-advertising sweep in CFPB history was the **2020 sweep of nine companies that mailed deceptive VA-guaranteed loan advertisements to military servicemembers and veterans.** All nine used direct-mail campaigns. All nine were charged with **violating the CFPA's UDAAP prohibition, the MAP Rule, and Regulation Z.** Each consent order recites that the Bureau "commenced this sweep in response to concerns about potentially unlawful advertising in the market that the VA identified."

The complete list, with dates, dockets, and civil money penalties, is:

### 2.3 RMK Financial Corp. d/b/a Majestic Home Loans (February 27, 2023) — repeat offender

- **Forum / docket:** Administrative Proceeding, Docket 2023-CFPB-0002. (Status: "Post Order/Post Judgment.")
- **Press release:** "**CFPB Shuts Down Mortgage Loan Business of RMK Financial for Repeat Offenses Against Military Families**" (Feb. 27, 2023).
- **Findings:** After the 2015 consent order, RMK disseminated **millions** of mortgage advertisements to military families that made deceptive representations or contained inadequate or impermissible disclosures in violation of the 2015 order, the **CFPA, the MAP Rule (Regulation N), and Regulation Z.** The Bureau found RMK advertisements that:
  - **"Tricked military families about the government's role"** — misrepresented that RMK was, or was affiliated with, the VA or the FHA; misrepresented that the VA or FHA sent the notices; or misrepresented that the advertised loans were provided by the VA or FHA.
  - **"Deceived borrowers about interest rates and key terms"** — illegally disclosed a simple annual interest rate more conspicuously than the APR; illegally advertised unavailable credit terms; and used the name of the homeowner's current lender in a misleading way.
  - **"Falsely misrepresented loan requirements and lied about projected savings from refinancing"** — represented that VA/FHA benefits were time limited; represented that VA cash-out refinancing loans were available without an appraisal, without an appraisal cost, without a minimum credit score, and without income verification; and misrepresented monthly payments, annual savings, and cash available.
- **Remedy:** **Permanent ban from the mortgage lending business** (advertising, marketing, promoting, offering, providing, originating, administering, servicing, or selling mortgage loans, or otherwise participating in or receiving remuneration from mortgage lending, or assisting others in doing so) **and a $1 million civil money penalty** deposited in the CFPB's victims relief fund.

The 2023 RMK order is the most important example in the CFPB's MAP Rule docket of the "repeat offender" theory of harm. The press release expressly says: "Today's action is one in a series of actions the CFPB is taking to halt repeat offenders, particularly those that violate agency and court orders. The CFPB recently proposed a registry to detect repeat offenders in the financial marketplace."

### 2.4 Where RMK maps onto §1014.3

Combining the 2015 and 2023 RMK orders, the Bureau charged misrepresentations in at least the following MAP Rule sub-paragraphs:

- **§1014.3(b)** — misrepresentations about interest rates and the use of a simple annual interest rate more conspicuously than the APR (Reg Z overlap).
- **§1014.3(g)** — using the word "fixed" or related representations to mislead about variability.
- **§1014.3(h)** — comparisons between a limited-period rate and the actual rate.
- **§1014.3(j)** — misrepresentations of cash available and savings from refinancing.
- **§1014.3(k)** — misrepresentations about the existence, number, amount, or timing of required payments.
- **§1014.3(n)** — misrepresentations that the provider is, or is affiliated with, the VA or FHA, or that the product is endorsed or sponsored by a government program, including "through the use of formats, symbols, or logos that resemble those of such entity."
- **§1014.3(o)** — misrepresentations about the source of the commercial communication (misusing the homeowner's current lender's name; implying the VA or FHA sent the notice).

## 3. Verbatim CFPB consent order language citing 12 CFR 1014.3 and 1014.4

Below is the operative language from the consent orders themselves, with each quotation keyed to the §1014.3 sub-paragraph the Bureau charged. Every quote in this section is from the underlying consent order or complaint, retrieved from the CFPB's files.consumerfinance.gov archive.

### 3.1 The 2015 RMK consent order (Docket 2015-CFPB-0007) — the first standalone MAP Rule case

The 2015 RMK order was structured as a single, simple "Findings and Conclusions" recitation rather than separate MAP Rule counts. It frames the central misrepresentation as a violation of both §1036 (CFPA deception) and §1014.3 (Regulation N).

> **¶ 14.** Respondent's mortgage advertisements used the names, logos, and seals of the VA and FHA, as well as other design elements, in such a manner that the net impression of the advertisements was misleading, giving the false impression that the advertisements were sent, endorsed, or sponsored by a U.S. government agency.

> **¶ 15.** The advertisements for VA-guaranteed refinance loans featured a large VA seal on the top of the advertisement, along with the VA logo and the words "Department of Veteran Affairs" in the top middle.

> **¶ 19.** Respondent never had a "VA Interest Rate Reduction Department."

> **¶ 22.** Below the advertised phone number, the FHA advertisements referred to the "FHA Streamline Department."

> **¶ 23.** Respondent never had an "FHA Streamline Department."

> **¶ 25.** Because of these various design elements, the net impressions created by the advertisements were likely to mislead reasonable consumers about the source of the advertisements, and, in particular, whether they were sent by a government agency.

> **¶ 26.** The net impressions created by the advertisements were also likely to mislead reasonable consumers about whether Respondent was or was affiliated with the FHA or VA.

> **¶ 27.** Finally, the net impressions created by the advertisements were likely to mislead reasonable consumers about whether the mortgages being offered were related to a government benefit or were endorsed or sponsored by a government program.

> **¶ 33.** Regulation N, 12 C.F.R. § 1014.3, prohibits any person from making "any material misrepresentation, expressly or by implication, in any commercial communication, regarding any term of any mortgage credit product."

> **¶ 36.** Thus, Respondent's representations, as described in Paragraph 34, constitute: (a) deceptive acts or practices in violation of sections 1031(a) and 1036(a)(1)(B) of the CFPA, 12 U.S.C. §§ 5531(a), 5536(a)(1)(B); and (b) material misrepresentations, in a commercial communication, regarding the terms of a mortgage credit product, in violation of Regulation N, 12 C.F.R. § 1014.3.

The 2015 order also captured the variable-rate / "fixed" misrepresentation:

> **¶ 37.** The bulk of Respondent's mailers advertised variable-rate mortgage loans, but they failed to state that the advertised rates were variable, except in small print on the outside of the mailer—the back of the advertisement.

> **¶ 42.** In fact, the fixed-rate loans offered by Respondent typically had higher interest rates than the variable rates advertised on Respondent's mailers.

> **¶ 47.** Thus, Respondent's representations, as described in Paragraph 45 constitute: (a) deceptive acts or practices in violation of sections 1031(a) and 1036(a)(1)(B) of the CFPA, 12 U.S.C. §§ 5531(a), 5536(a)(1)(B); and (b) material misrepresentations, in a commercial communication, regarding the terms of a mortgage credit product, in violation of Regulation N, 12 C.F.R. § 1014.3.

### 3.2 The 2020 VA-mortgage-mailer sweep — the operative §1014.3 charges

The 2020 sweep consent orders use a much more granular "**X. Misrepresentations About Y, 12 C.F.R. § 1014.3(z)**" template, with one paragraph stating the rule and one paragraph applying it. Below are the verbatim counts from three of the orders that show the full range of MAP Rule subsections the Bureau charges in a mortgage-advertising case.

#### 3.2.1 Sovereign Lending Group, Inc. (Docket 2020-BCFP-0005, July 24, 2020)

The Sovereign order is the most fully-developed MAP Rule template. Verbatim counts:

> **¶ 86.** Under 12 C.F.R. § 1014.3(b), it is a violation for any person subject to the MAP Rule to make any misrepresentation, directly or indirectly, expressly or by implication, in any commercial communication, about "[t]he annual percentage rate, simple annual rate, periodic rate, or any other rate" applicable to a mortgage credit product. Under 12 C.F.R. § 1014.3, such a misrepresentation is specifically prohibited and is therefore material.
>

### 3.4 Where the Bureau has NOT charged §1014.4 ("Waiver not permitted")

The Bureau has charged **§1014.4** in only a small number of consent orders (where the respondent obtained or attempted to obtain a consumer waiver of MAP Rule rights). The 2015 RMK order includes a prohibition parallel to §1014.4 but does not cite it as a separate violation count; the 2020 sweep and 2023 RMK orders likewise focus on §1014.3 and §1036. The standard "prohibition on waivers" injunction language appears in the conduct-relief sections of the 2015 and 2023 RMK orders (and in the conduct-relief sections of the 2020 sweep orders), and the consent order for **PHH Mortgage** (a non-MAP case) is the one that most clearly recites a §1014.4-style waiver count in its operative paragraphs.

## 4. Specific deceptive claims the Bureau has charged in MAP Rule / mortgage-advertising cases

Mapping the recurring claim language in the consent orders back to §1014.3 (or to Reg Z where it overlaps):

### 4.1 "Government insured" / "Government affiliated" (false)

- **§1014.3(n)** — primary count in every 2020 sweep case and in RMK 2015 and RMK 2023. The order language tracks the regulation: "**association** of the mortgage credit product or any provider of such product with any other person or program, including but not limited to misrepresentations that: (1) The provider is, or is affiliated with, any governmental entity or other organization; or (2) The product is or relates to a government benefit, or is endorsed, sponsored by, or affiliated with any government or other program, including but not limited to through the use of formats, symbols, or logos that resemble those of such entity, organization, or program."
- **Specific representations cited as deceptive:**
  - "Advertisements that misrepresented that RMK was, or was affiliated with, the VA or the FHA, that the VA or FHA sent the notices, or that the advertised loans were provided by the VA or FHA" (RMK 2023).
  - VA and FHA logos and seals in mailers that "falsely implied that RMK was affiliated with the government" (RMK 2023; also RMK 2015).
  - Use of the "VA" or "FHA" name, seal, or logo, or government-style formatting, in the 2020 sweep (Sovereign, Prime Choice, Go Direct, Hypotec, Service 1st).
- **Adjacent CFPB theory (not 1014.3 but co-charged):** FDIC-name/logo misrepresentations are covered by **CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-02: Deceptive representations involving the FDIC's name or logo or deposit insurance** (May 17, 2022; 87 FR 30282). The circular says a person "likely violates the CFPA's prohibition on deception if they misuse the name or logo of the FDIC or engage in false advertising or make misrepresentations to consumers about deposit insurance, regardless of whether such conduct (including the misrepresentation of insured status) is engaged in knowingly." It expressly notes that "firms offering or providing digital assets, including crypto-assets, may be particularly prone to making such deceptive claims to consumers about FDIC deposit insurance coverage." The circular is structured around 12 U.S.C. §5536 (CFPA deception) and is not itself a 1014.3 count, but the misrepresentation it describes (e.g., "FDIC-insured" when not) would also be a §1014.3(n) misrepresentation if the product advertised is a mortgage credit product.

### 4.2 "Approved" / "Pre-approved" used misleadingly

- **§1014.3(q)** and **§1014.3(r)** — primary counts. The regulation uses the exact words "**preapproved or guaranteed for any such product or term**" and "**preapproved or guaranteed for any such refinancing or modification**."
- **Specific representations cited as deceptive:**
  - Sovereign Lending Group — "**falsely stated that the consumer had been prequalified** for the advertised mortgage" (Sovereign ¶ 42, ¶ 97).
  - Prime Choice Funding — same: "**falsely stated that the consumer had been prequalified** for the advertised mortgage."
  - RMK 2023 — "**the phrase 'PREQUALIFICATION NOTICE,' which was capitalized and boxed to draw attention to it, falsely implied that Respondent was, or was affiliated with, the FHA or another government agency**" (¶ 28–29). RMK also violated §1014.3(q) by representing that "income verification or a minimum credit score were not required to obtain a VA-guaranteed cash-out refinance mortgage" (¶¶ 101–102).
- **CFPB consumer education backdrop:** the Ask CFPB page "What's the difference between a prequalification letter and a preapproval letter?" (last reviewed Dec. 5, 2023) tells consumers: "Prequalification and preapproval letters both specify how much the lender is willing to lend to you, up to a certain amount and based on certain assumptions. These letters provide useful information about your likelihood of getting a loan but are not guaranteed loan offers." It also says: "Don't worry about which word lenders use. Lenders' processes vary widely, and the words they use don't tell you much about a particular lender's process even if it may result in legal differences." (See `cfpb_prequal_vs_preapproval.txt`.) The consumer page effectively operationalizes the §1014.3(q) standard: a representation that the consumer has been "preapproved" or "prequalified" is deceptive unless the lender has actually verified and committed under conditions consistent with that label.
- **Other MAP Rule §1014.3(q)/(r) "ability or likelihood" representations** the Bureau has charged: representations that "consumers with FICO scores as low as 500 would qualify for the advertised rates" (Go Direct Lenders) and representations that "no minimum credit score and no income verification were required to qualify for VA cash-out refinancing loans" (RMK 2023). These cross §1014.3(n), (q), and (r) — they simultaneously inflate the consumer's likelihood of obtaining the product and use language that conveys implicit government sponsorship.

### 4.3 "Lowest payment" / "lowest rate" / "best rate"

- **§1014.3(b)** (rate misrepresentations), **§1014.3(h)** (rate/payment comparisons), and **§1014.3(k)** (minimum or required payment misrepresentations). RMK 2023 also charged via **§1014.3(j)** (amount of obligation) when the misrepresentation concerned the monthly payment amount.
- **Specific representations cited as deceptive:**
  - RMK 2023 — "**Deceived borrowers about interest rates and key terms**: RMK's advertisements illegally disclosed a simple annual interest rate more conspicuously than the annual percentage rate, **illegally advertised unavailable credit terms**, and used the name of the homeowner's current lender in a misleading way."
  - RMK 2023 — "**Falsely misrepresented … the amount of monthly payments**, the annual savings under the advertised loans, and the cash available in connection with the advertised loans."
  - Sovereign / Prime Choice — "**misleadingly described an adjustable-rate mortgage as having a 'fixed' rate**" (this is a §1014.3(g) violation that is also a comparator claim under §1014.3(h)).
  - Hypotec — "**advertised specific credit terms, such as interest rates, APRs, and hypothetical payment amounts that it was not prepared to offer, or that it could only offer for an introductory period but advertised as if they were permanent loan terms**." Service 1st had the same set of misrepresentations.
  - Low VA Rates — "**used misleading descriptions of rates**" and "**1,141,690 mailers to consumers between January 1, 2016, and September 16, 2016, that prominently referred to a 1.75% fixed simple annual interest rate**" when in fact the 1.75% fixed rate was not available.
  - Go Direct Lenders — "**2.75% interest rate and 2.885% APR were available to borrowers with 'FICO scores as low as 500.' But as indicated in the fine print, the advertised interest rate and APR were not available to borrowers with a credit score below 740**."
- **Bureau theory of harm:** these are not "puffery" claims; the Bureau treats them as material because consumers select among competing offers on rate and payment, and a misrepresentation here changes the comparison. The Regulation Z overlap matters because "fixed" rate and APR presentation are governed by §1026.24 (advertisements).

### 4.4 "Save" / "savings" claims

- **§1014.3(h)** (rate/payment comparisons) and **§1014.3(j)** (amount of obligation or credit available). The New Day USA "net benefit" worksheet case charges the same general theory (deceptive comparison of "previous" vs. "new" monthly mortgage payment) under the CFPA.
- **Specific representations cited as deceptive:**
  - Low VA Rates — "**used misleading representations regarding the savings or financial benefits available to consumers**" and "**promoted 'YOUR 1 Year Savings' beside an estimated payment on the front of the ad . . . . [I]n fact, the touted 'savings' were not as advertised and resulted from deferring payments consumers would still ultimately be obligated to make**" (Low VA Rates ¶¶ 49–51).
  - Hypotec — "stated that it would pay an estimated escrow refund of a specific amount if the consumer refinanced through Hypotec, even though **the advertised escrow refund amount was calculated using a method that had no bearing on the actual escrow refund amount, and customers were required to fund a new escrow account upon generating a new loan**."
  - Service 1st — "**represented that a consumer could '[s]kip two payments' or 'miss' two payments by refinancing with the company, but it did not disclose the limitations on this option, or that the skipped or missed payments would be added to the principal balance of the consumer's loan**." This is a §1014.3(j) misrepresentation of the obligation and a §1014.3(k) misrepresentation about the existence or amount of required payments.
  - RMK 2023 — "**misrepresented … the annual savings under the advertised loans, and the cash available in connection with the advertised loans**." RMK was also charged with an "**unsubstantiated representations**" count for lacking a reasonable basis for the savings claim.
  - Hypotec and Service 1st — both used the false "**the Economic Stimulus Program will end soon. There is currently no plan to extend the Stimulus Program**" statement. This is a §1014.3(n) misrepresentation because the statement creates the false impression that a government benefit is time-limited; the Bureau's narrative framing also makes clear that the Bureau views the statement as a §1014.3(n) violation premised on the false government-affiliation suggestion.

### 4.5 Misrepresentations about credit qualification

Already covered above (Go Direct, RMK). The "FICO as low as 500 would qualify" line and the "no minimum credit score, no income verification, no appraisal" lines are the cleanest examples of §1014.3(q)/(r) misrepresentations. They are also material to the consumer's decision to apply.

## 5. Lead-generation websites as "advertisements" under the MAP Rule (and adjacent CFPB theories)

The CFPB has not brought a standalone "MAP Rule" case naming a lead-generator website. But the Bureau has, in related actions and guidance, treated lead-generator websites as commercial communications and has used 1014.3-style misrepresentation theories against them in adjacent counts (UDAAP, GLBA, FDCPA, EFTA, TILA/Regulation Z, FCRA). Two pieces of work are central.

### 5.1 CFPB v. D & D Marketing, Inc. d/b/a T3Leads, et al. (the "lead aggregator" case closest to "Romeo and Liliana" in flavor)

- **Docket:** Civil Action No. 2:15-cv-09692 (C.D. Cal., filed Dec. 17, 2015).
- **Press release:** "CFPB Takes Action Against Lead Aggregators for Online Trafficking of Personal Information" (Dec. 17, 2015) and the follow-on "CFPB Takes Action Against Co-Founders of Online Lead Aggregator" (Apr. 21, 2016).
- **Allegations (per the CFPB press releases, which are the public summaries):** T3Leads, a lead aggregator based in Burbank, California, and owned by Grigor and Marina Demirchyan, bought and resold consumer loan applications. The complaint alleged T3Leads **"ignored false or misleading statements about lenders obtaining consumer applications"** — specifically that lead generators "often falsely claimed to match consumers with lenders that 'follow the rules' or offer 'reasonable' terms" — and "**failed to vet or monitor purchasers**," and "**steered consumers toward unfavorable loans**." The follow-on actions against T3Leads co-founders Dmitry Fomichev and Davit (David) Gasparyan alleged they "**provided substantial assistance**" to T3Leads. The separate action against Eric V. Sancho d/b/a Lead Publisher (Docket 2015-CFPB-0033) ordered $21,151 in disgorgement and **permanently banned Sancho from the financial products and online consumer leads industries** for selling roughly 3 million leads to debt collectors who used them to harass consumers for debts they did not actually owe.
- **T3Leads complaint language (verbatim, from the filed complaint at C.D. Cal. 2:15-cv-09692):**
  > **¶ 14.** T3 regularly purchases leads from lead generators whose websites include misrepresentations that are likely to mislead consumers into believing that lenders in T3's network have been evaluated and meet certain standards.
  > **¶ 15.** Lead generators have incorrectly represented that they were themselves lenders. Lead generators also falsely suggested that they would help consumers find the best rates or lowest fees or that they would review consumers' applications to match them with appropriate lenders.
  > **¶ 17.** T3 does not know the loan terms that consumers will be offered by its lead purchasers, and T3 makes no attempt to match consumers with the best loan for their needs, as consumers are led to believe by lead generators.
  > **¶ 28.** T3's lead generators make statements to consumers regarding the lenders that will receive the consumers' information and the loans those lenders will offer.
  > **¶ 29.** T3 knows or should know of the lead generators' statements and that they are often false and misleading.
  > **¶ 30.** T3 does not vet or monitor its lead purchasers for illegal activity.
  > **¶ 39.** An act or practice is abusive if it "takes unreasonable advantage of . . . a lack of understanding on the part of the consumer of the material risks, costs, or conditions of the product or service." 12 U.S.C. § 5531(d)(2)(A).
  > **¶ 40.** Contrary to representations by lead generators, consumers are likely to be steered, through T3's ping tree, to lenders offering less-favorable terms than may otherwise be available to them. . . . Consumers also are likely to be steered to lenders that offer contracts providing for the application of tribal law to the contract and providing an exclusive tribal dispute-resolution process.
- **Theoretical placement of these claims in the MAP Rule:** The lead-generator's website is a "commercial communication" under 1014.2 ("any written or oral statement, illustration, or depiction, whether in English or any other language, that is designed to effect a sale or create interest in purchasing goods or services, whether it appears on … the internet, cellular network, or any other medium. Promotional materials and items and Web pages are included in the term 'commercial communication.'"). A representation on the lead-generator's website that the consumer will be matched with "reasonable" or "rule-following" lenders, when the lead generator is in fact routing the consumer to higher-cost tribal or unlicensed lenders, is a **material misrepresentation in a commercial communication about the terms of mortgage credit products** and is squarely within the text of **§1014.3(n), (o), and (q)**. The Bureau charged it instead as a UDAAP under §1036, presumably because (a) the Bureau was not yet confident about applying 1014.3 outside the mortgage lender / broker setting and (b) the UDAAP count allowed it to reach the lead aggregator (a "service provider" under §1031) without proving that T3Leads itself was a "person subject to" Part 1014 in the same way a mortgage lender is.

### 5.2 CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-02 — FDIC name/logo (May 17, 2022; 87 FR 30282)

- **Press release:** "CFPB Warns Consumers about Companies Misusing the FDIC's Name or Logo" (May 17, 2022).
- **Direct application to lead generators and websites:** The circular does not name lead generators, but it states that a covered person or service provider "likely violates the CFPA's prohibition on deception if they misuse the name or logo of the FDIC or engage in false advertising or make misrepresentations to consumers about deposit insurance, regardless of whether such conduct (including the misrepresentation of insured status) is engaged in knowingly." It is the operative CFPB guidance on unauthorized use of a regulator's name or logo in advertising, and it is frequently cited by the Bureau alongside MAP Rule counts when a respondent's website or mailer uses government branding.

### 5.3 CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2024-01 — Preferencing and steering practices by digital intermediaries (Feb. 29, 2024; 89 FR 16526)

- **Press release:** "CFPB Takes Action to Ensure Consumers Can Compare Banking, Credit Card, and Other Financial Products" (Feb. 29, 2024).
- **Operative theory (verbatim, from the circular):**
  > **Question presented:** Can operators of digital comparison-shopping tools or lead generators violate the Consumer Financial Protection Act (CFPA) by preferencing products or services based on financial or other benefits to the operator?
  >
  > **Response:** Yes. Operators of digital comparison-shopping tools can violate the prohibition on abusive acts or practices if they distort the shopping experience by steering consumers to certain products or services based on remuneration to the operator. Similarly, lead generators can violate the prohibition on abusive practices if they steer consumers to one participating financial services provider instead of another based on compensation received. Where consumers reasonably rely on an operator of a digital comparison-shopping tool or a lead generator to act in their interests, the operator or lead generator can take unreasonable advantage of that reliance by giving preferential treatment to their own or other products or services through steering or enhanced product placement, for financial or other benefits.
  >
  > The circular then enumerates **non-exhaustive examples** including:
  > - "A tool operator routinely matches consumers with a loan provider because it pays the highest fee per application."
  > - "A tool operator receives different payment based on whether the digital comparison-shopping tool meets a certain threshold volume allocation of leads generated within a set period of time, and uses steering practices to increase the likelihood the operator will satisfy volume allocation requirements."
  > - "A tool operator or lead generator uses dynamic bidding or a bounty system to determine which offers are presented to consumers with certain demographic or other characteristics."
  > - "A lead generator guarantees a certain number and quality of leads to multiple participating lenders and divides customers meeting those criteria up without regard to the fact that consumers with similar characteristics are receiving different offers."
- **CFPB's companion 2022 interpretive rule on digital marketing providers:** **Limited Applicability of Consumer Financial Protection Act's "Time or Space" Exception with Respect to Digital Marketing Providers**, 87 FR 50556 (Aug. 17, 2022). This pulls a category of lead-generator-like digital marketers into CFPA coverage as "service providers," which in turn allows UDAAP counts. (It is also referenced in the 2024 circular.)
- **CFPB companion 2023 advisory opinion on digital mortgage comparison-shopping platforms:** **Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (Regulation X); Digital Mortgage Comparison-Shopping Platforms and Related Payments to Operators**, 88 FR 9162 (Feb. 13, 2023). This is an **advisory opinion under RESPA Section 8**, not the MAP Rule, but it is the closest analogue for the issue of payments from lenders to comparison-shopping platforms; the opinion advises that, all else equal, "presenting a greater number of comparison options rather than fewer" generally reduces RESPA Section 8 risk.
- **Placement of lead generators in the MAP Rule framework:** The 2024 circular is the strongest existing CFPB document arguing that lead generators and comparison-shopping websites can be "covered persons" under §1031 and subject to UDAAP. **As of late 2024, the Bureau has not used a standalone §1014.3 misrepresentation count against a lead generator's website.** A litigator wanting to bring a 1014.3 case against a lead generator would have to clear three threshold issues that the T3Leads order did not reach: (i) that the website is a "commercial communication" regarding a "term" of a "mortgage credit product" within the meaning of 1014.2 and 1014.3; (ii) that the lead generator is a "person" over which the FTC has jurisdiction under the FTC Act (and therefore "any person" within 1014.3); and (iii) that the misrepresentation is "material." The text of the regulation arguably supports each of these, but no public CFPB filing yet confirms it.

### 5.4 Cases involving lead generators using bank/credit union names without authorization

I was not able to locate a 2022–2023 CFPB enforcement action against a lead generator that specifically used a bank or credit union's name in its advertising, comparable to the FTC's Career Education Corporation matter (which involved false claims of affiliation with the U.S. military) or the FTC's ITMedia Solutions matter (which involved a "loan application" form that misrepresented who would receive the consumer's information). The closest CFPB materials are:

- **CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-02 (FDIC name/logo)** is the closest Bureau document on bank/credit-union-name misuse in advertising. Combined with the 2024 circular, it establishes that (a) using the FDIC's name or logo in a mortgage-advertising context is a CFPA deception that would also support a 1014.3(n) count if the product is a mortgage credit product; and (b) a lead generator that claims to be matching the consumer to "reasonable" or "rule-following" lenders is exposed to a UDAAP count, and to a 1014.3(n)/(o) count if the representation is material and ties to a mortgage credit product.
- **CFPB v. D & D Marketing d/b/a T3Leads** is the operative CFPB precedent on lead generators that use lender-side representations ("reasonable," "follow the rules") to mislead consumers about who would see their application. The T3Leads complaint does not specifically allege that the lead generator used a particular bank or credit union's name, but the Bureau's theory would support such a count under either UDAAP or (in a future case) §1014.3(o).
- **FTC v. ITMedia Solutions LLC**, No. 2:22-cv-00073 (C.D. Cal. Jan. 5, 2022), and **FTC v. Blue Global, LLC**, No. 2:17-cv-2117 (D. Ariz. July 3, 2017), and **FTC v. Career Education Corporation**, No. 1:19-cv-05739 (N.D. Ill. Aug. 27, 2019) — each cited in **CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2024-01** as examples of lead generators that used unauthorized institutional names or made false "matching" claims. These are FTC matters rather than CFPB matters, but the CFPB is signaling that the same conduct, when involving consumer financial products or services, will be charged under §1031.

If "GoFastBaby" or a similar named company is a real lead-generator advertiser, the closest public CFPB action on a comparable fact pattern is the **CFPB Circular 2024-01** enforcement theory combined with the T3Leads §1036 count pattern. As of the cutoff of this research I have not been able to identify a public CFPB enforcement action against a lead generator that used a specific bank or credit union's name; if a confidential CID or a non-public action exists, it is not on the CFPB's public docket.

## 6. The CFPB's "repeat offender" framework as it applies to MAP Rule violators

The CFPB's "repeat offender" framework is the central theme of Director Chopra's 2022–2023 enforcement program. It is not a single "report" but a multi-document body of work. I have reconstructed what the user appears to be asking about by reading the relevant primary documents.

### 6.1 What the September 2022 "report" actually was

The CFPB's principal September 2022 publication was the **September 15, 2022** report titled **"Buy Now, Pay Later: Market trends and consumer impacts."** The press release announcing the report is dated **SEP 15, 2022**, and the report's key finding is that the five BNPL lenders surveyed originated 180 million loans totaling over $24 billion in 2021, a near tenfold increase from 2019. The report identifies three categories of potential consumer risks:

> "Inconsistent consumer protections: Borrowers seeking Buy Now, Pay Later credit may encounter products that do not offer protections that are standard elsewhere in the consumer financial marketplace. These include a lack of standardized cost-of-credit disclosures, minimal dispute resolution rights, a forced opt-in to autopay, and companies that assess multiple late fees on the same missed payment.
>
> Data harvesting and monetization: Many Buy Now, Pay Later lenders are shifting their business models toward proprietary app usage, which allows them to build a valuable digital profile of each user's shopping preferences and behavior. . . .
>
> Debt accumulation and overextension: Buy Now, Pay Later is engineered to encourage consumers to purchase more and borrow more. . . . borrowers can easily end up taking out several loans within a short time frame at multiple lenders or Buy Now, Pay Later debts may have effects on other debts."

The BNPL report **does not contain a section on repeat corporate offenders** in the regulatory sense. The report uses the term "repeat" only to discuss consumer repeat-usage of BNPL (e.g., "repeat usage is a key metric for BNPL lenders' top-line growth," "repeat BNPL usage—enhanced by the 'home field advantage' features in lenders' proprietary [apps]," "80% increase in repeat customer rate"). It is not about repeat-offender companies. The closest adjacent CFPB publication in September 2022 was the CFPB's announcement of the **proposed repeat-offender registry** (described below), not a research report.

### 6.2 The "Repeat Offender Unit" and proposed registry (the actual CFPB repeat-offender work)

- The CFPB has, on its public website, a page titled **"CFPB's Repeat Offender Unit"** (https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/supervision-examinations/cfpbs-repeat-offender-unit/, as listed in search results). The page states: "The CFPB established the Repeat Offender Unit in 2022 and a registry to detect and deter repeat offenders in 2024. It is critical to ensure that large market actors not treat fines and penalties as a cost of doing business. The Repeat Offender Unit is a specialized team that is responsible for ensuring that supervised entities comply with all requirements of CFPB law enforcement orders." (Direct fetch of the page returned a 404 at the time of this research; the description above is the search-engine excerpt.)
- On **December 6, 2022**, the CFPB **announced a proposed repeat-offender registry** that would have required nonbank covered institutions to submit information about their prior CFPB and other federal- and state-agency orders. The proposal drew heavy comment and, per a December 12, 2022 Bloomberg article reposted by Goodwin, "sparks concerns of overreach." The proposed rule was not finalized before the change of administration in early 2025.
- The unit's public work has included the **Feb. 27, 2023 RMK Financial / Majestic Home Loans order** (the permanent ban and $1 million penalty described in §2.3 above), which is the lead case demonstrating how the unit uses MAP Rule + Regulation Z + the 2015 order to escalate a repeat offender to a permanent industry bar.
- A non-CFPB summary of the unit's recent work is the Consumer Federation of America's October 15, 2025 document "CFPB Pending Enforcement Actions" (https://www.consumerfed.org/media/legacy/10.15.25-CFPB-Repeat-Offender-Enforcement-Cases.pdf), which lists pending cases against repeat offenders including ACE Cash Express, Vanderbilt Mortgage, RMK Financial, and others.

### 6.3 What the CFPB's repeat-offender work says about MAP Rule violators (the closest available answer)

The CFPB's MAP Rule docket contains a small number of repeat-offender candidates:

- **RMK Financial / Majestic Home Loans** is the clearest repeat-offender MAP Rule case. The 2015 order was based on materially the same conduct (deceptive government-affiliation claims, false interest rates, and false payment amounts) that the Bureau charged again in the 2023 order. The 2023 press release expressly invokes the repeat-offender framework: "Today's action is one in a series of actions the CFPB is taking to halt repeat offenders, particularly those that violate agency and court orders."
- The 2020 VA-mortgage-mailer sweep was not framed as a repeat-offender initiative, but the Bureau's 2020 orders include ongoing compliance requirements (recordkeeping, monitoring, training) for each respondent, and the 2023 RMK order expressly cited the existence of the sweep as part of the CFPB's enforcement landscape: "The CFPB has previously warned about VA home loan scams. Many servicemembers, veterans, and military spouses receive fraudulent calls and mailers from companies claiming to be affiliated with the government, the VA, or their home loan servicer."
- **Prospect Mortgage, LLC** (2017 RESPA order, $3.5 million CMP) and **New Day USA** (2024 net-benefit worksheet case, $2.25 million CMP) are repeat-offender-adjacent, though neither is a MAP Rule count.
- **ACE Cash Express, Inc.** is a CFPB repeat-offender cash-advances case (sued in 2014) that the consumer-federation 2025 document describes as having been "sued by the CFPB for concealing no-cost repayment plans and improperly withdrawing consumers' funds. The repeat offender kept borrowers in debt and in the dark, generating at least $240 million in reborrowing fees." ACE Cash Express is not a MAP Rule case; it is cited here only to illustrate the repeat-offender framework the Bureau is applying across its docket.
- The post-January 2025 CFPB has emphasized a different enforcement posture. The **2025 Enforcement lookback** page states: "The CFPB is now focusing its enforcement resources on: pressing threats to consumers, particularly servicemembers and their families, and veterans; actual consumer fraud where there are identifiable victims with material and measurable consumer damages as opposed to matters based on the perception that consumers made 'wrong' choices; . . . areas that are clearly within the CFPB's statutory authority and not pursuing matters under novel legal theories . . . ." MAP Rule cases (RMK 2015, RMK 2023, the 2020 VA-mailer sweep) squarely fit within the "servicemembers and veterans" and "actual consumer fraud" priorities; the post-2025 posture of the Bureau should not affect the doctrinal standing of those prior orders.

## 7. CFPB compliance bulletins, circulars, and guides that bear on the MAP Rule

The CFPB's "Guidance" page (https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/guidance/) lists the current and withdrawn guidance documents. The MAP Rule itself has not been the subject of a stand-alone CFPB compliance guide as of the cutoff of this research; the closest is the FTC's 2011 Statement of Basis and Purpose accompanying the MAP Rule (the FTC and CFPB concurrent rule). CFPB mortgage-advertising guidance is delivered through the following instruments:

### 7.1 CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-02 (May 17, 2022) — FDIC name/logo / deposit insurance

- **Operative proposition:** a covered person or service provider "likely violates the CFPA's prohibition on deception" if it misuses the FDIC's name or logo, or makes misrepresentations about deposit insurance, regardless of intent. The circular is a guidepost for any MAP Rule §1014.3(n) count where the misrepresentation is "FDIC-insured" or "federally insured."

### 7.2 CFPB Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2024-01 (Feb. 29, 2024) — Lead generators and digital comparison-shopping tools

- **Operative proposition:** lead generators and digital comparison-shopping tools can be "covered persons" under §1031(d)(2)(C) and can violate the **abuse** prohibition by steering consumers to products based on compensation to the operator. The circular is not a 1014.3 count, but it is the current CFPB roadmap for treating lead-generator websites as advertising and the most relevant CFPB guidance for any future 1014.3 action against a lead generator.

### 7.3 CFPB Supervisory Highlights (multiple issues)

- The CFPB's **Supervisory Highlights** reports the Bureau's findings from supervisory examinations, including specific examples of MAP Rule / Reg Z advertising violations at supervised banks and nonbanks. The most cited and most relevant for this purpose are the issues from **Fall 2022 (Issue 28)**, **Spring 2023 (Issue 29)**, **Fall 2023 (Issue 30)**, **Winter 2024 (Issue 31)**, **Spring 2024 (Issue 32)**, **Summer 2024 (Issue 33)**, **Fall 2024 (Issue 34)**, **Winter 2025 (Issue 35)**, and **Spring 2025 (Issue 36)**. The 2024 mortgage advertising issues contain extensive discussion of red-flag review triggers for "lowest rate," "no income verification," "no appraisal," and "guaranteed approval" claims — the same language the Bureau has charged in RMK 2015, RMK 2023, and the 2020 sweep.

### 7.4 CFPB Bulletin 2012-05 (Apr. 19, 2012) and 2013-12 (Oct. 15, 2013) — SAFE Act / Mortgage Loan Originator transition; mortgage-servicing implementation

- Not MAP Rule; included only to flag that the Bureau's mortgage-advertising compliance framework is delivered primarily through bulletins and circulars rather than a stand-alone MAP Rule guide.

### 7.5 2014 FTC/CFPB MAP Rule outreach materials (legacy)

- The FTC and CFPB published consumer-education materials at the time the MAP Rule took effect. The consumer-facing version is preserved in the FTC's "Mortgage Matters" series. The most recent FTC consumer-ed update predates the 2021 reorganization; the CFPB has not separately republished a stand-alone MAP Rule consumer education page, and Ask CFPB pages on mortgage advertising route through Reg Z / mortgage servicing rather than a stand-alone MAP Rule topic.

### 7.6 Advisory opinions and interpretive rules relevant to mortgage advertising

- **Advisory Opinion 2023-01 (RESPA Reg X) — Digital Mortgage Comparison-Shopping Platforms and Related Payments to Operators**, 88 FR 9162 (Feb. 13, 2023). RESPA, not MAP, but directly relevant to how comparison-shopping websites and lead generators are regulated when the advertised product is a mortgage credit product. The opinion advises that, all else equal, "presenting a greater number of comparison options rather than fewer" generally reduces Section 8 risk. (The CFPB expressly references this opinion in Circular 2024-01.)
- **Interpretive Rule, 87 FR 50556 (Aug. 17, 2022) — Limited Applicability of CFPA's "Time or Space" Exception with Respect to Digital Marketing Providers.** This is the CFPB's interpretive rule on the "time or space" exception to the definition of "service provider" and is the principal CFPB authority for treating certain lead-generator-like digital marketers as CFPA service providers.

### 7.7 Bulletin on Government Impersonation (Apr. 27, 2022) — CFPB Bulletin 2022-03

- The CFPB has also issued consumer and industry guidance noting that the FTC's **Government and Business Impersonation Rule** (16 CFR Part 461, finalized Mar. 2024) reaches "government impersonator" and "business impersonator" schemes involving consumer financial products. The Bureau has not charged the MAP Rule and the FTC's impersonation rule together in the same case as of the cutoff, but the FTC's rule extends the same conduct theory to non-CFPB jurisdiction (e.g., mortgage closing scams).

## 8. Putting the 2020 sweep and the 2023 RMK order side by side

For a practitioner, the two operational cores of the Bureau's MAP Rule docket are the 2020 sweep consent orders and the 2023 RMK order. The pleading count patterns are very similar:

| Element of misrepresentation | 2020 sweep | RMK 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| False government affiliation / logos (§1014.3(n)) | ✓ (every order in the sweep) | ✓ (the lead count) |
| False interest rate / APR (§1014.3(b), Reg Z §1026.24) | ✓ (Hypotec, Service 1st, Low VA Rates) | ✓ |
| False payment / cash available / savings (§1014.3(j), (h)) | ✓ (Hypotec escrow refund, Service 1st "skip two payments", Low VA Rates savings) | ✓ |
| False ARM-as-"fixed" (§1014.3(g)) | ✓ (Sovereign, Prime Choice) | ✓ (implicit) |
| False "prequalified" / "preapproved" (§1014.3(q), (r)) | ✓ (Sovereign, Prime Choice) | implicit (the FICO-500 and no-income lines) |
| False appraisal / qualifying representations (§1014.3(q), (r), (n)) | ✓ (Go Direct) | ✓ (VA cash-out refi lines) |
| False "stimulus program will end soon" / "benefits time-limited" | ✓ (Hypotec, Service 1st) | ✓ ("benefits available to those who qualified for VA or FHA loans were time limited") |
| False source of communication / use of consumer's lender name (§1014.3(o)) | ✓ (Sovereign, Prime Choice) | ✓ |
| Regulation Z / TILA disclosure failures | ✓ (every order) | ✓ |

This is the working count template the Bureau would use in any new MAP Rule case.

## 9. Practical compliance takeaways for a mortgage advertiser / lender / broker

Drawing the regulatory text and the Bureau's charging record together:

- **Government affiliation / logos:** any use of the FHA, VA, USDA, or any federal or state program name, seal, or logo in mortgage advertising is presumptively scrutinized for §1014.3(n) compliance. The same is true of the FDIC name/logo under the Bureau's 2022 circular.
- **"Preapproved" / "prequalified":** these words have meaning under §1014.3(q)/(r) regardless of which one the lender uses. The compliance posture is the same: the lender must be able to substantiate the credit decision and the consumer's actual ability to obtain the product on the stated terms. The Bureau's consumer-education guidance treats the two as functionally interchangeable; a 1014.3 enforcement action will, too.
- **"Lowest rate" / "best rate" / "lowest payment" / "save" / "fixed":** treat these as material representations. The §1014.3(b) (rate), (g) (fixed), (h) (comparisons), (j) (cash available / savings), and (k) (minimum or required payments) counts are read together. Disclosure language alone will not cure a substantive misrepresentation. The Reg Z §1026.24 substantiation rules apply on top.
- **"No appraisal" / "no income verification" / "FICO 500":** these are §1014.3(q)/(r) misrepresentations about the consumer's ability or likelihood to obtain the product and §1014.3(n) misrepresentations about the product's character. They are not puffery; the Bureau has treated them as material in the 2020 sweep and in RMK 2023.
- **Source of communication:** the use of a consumer's current lender's or servicer's name, or any other representation that the communication is from the consumer's current lender or servicer, is §1014.3(o).
- **Lead-generator websites:** if your site is a "lead generator" or "digital comparison-shopping tool," you are a "covered person" under §1031 (Circular 2024-01) and a "service provider" under the 2022 digital-marketing-provider interpretive rule. Treat the website copy as advertising subject to the §1014.3 / Reg Z substantiation standards. The current enforcement theory is UDAAP under §1036, but a 1014.3 count is doctrinally available and the Bureau's 2024 guidance is signaling that the underlying misrepresentation theory (false "matching," false "reasonable lenders," false "follow the rules" representations) is the same.
- **Recordkeeping:** maintain for 24 months the commercial communications, sales scripts, training materials, and product/offer documentation that support the representations made (§1014.5). The Bureau's VA-mailer sweep consent orders include ongoing compliance requirements.
- **Waivers:** any attempt to obtain a consumer waiver of 1014.3 rights is itself a §1014.4 violation.
