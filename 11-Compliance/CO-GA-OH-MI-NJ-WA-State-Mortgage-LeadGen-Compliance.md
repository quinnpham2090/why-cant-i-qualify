# 1. COLORADO

## 1.1 Colorado Division of Real Estate and Division of Banking — mortgage licensing framework

**Lead regulators:**
- **Division of Real Estate (DRE)** — within the Colorado Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA) — is the regulator for **Mortgage Loan Originators** (MLOs) and **mortgage companies** under the Colorado Mortgage Loan Originator Licensing Act and the SAFE Act. Source: Colorado Division of Real Estate "Mortgage Loan Originator" program page, https://dre.colorado.gov/mortgage-loan-originator (last accessed 2025).
- **Division of Banking** within DORA supervises Colorado state-chartered banks, trust companies, money transmitters, and consumer-finance licensees (payday / installment lenders), but historically has **not** been the primary mortgage broker/lender regulator for non-bank entities; that role sits with DRE.
  - Source: DORA Divisions page, https://dora.colorado.gov/ ("Division of Real Estate" and "Division of Banking" entries).
- **Colorado Department of Law** (Office of the Attorney General) is the primary state-level consumer-protection enforcer (CPA, CCPA-equivalent Colorado Privacy Act, Colorado Consumer Protection Act).

**Statutory framework — historical and current:**

- **Title 12, Article 61 — "Mortgage Loan Originators"** (formerly "Loan Originators") — was the primary SAFE-Act-implementation statute. This is the citation referenced in older NMLS filings for Colorado MLOs.
- **SB 13-015 (2013)** recodified the Colorado Mortgage Brokerage Business Act and MLO law into Title 12, Articles 61 and 61.5.
- **SB 21-057 (2021)** — the "Colorado Student Loan Servicers Act" — is **not** a mortgage law but is cited here because it is the prior-year vehicle that created the Private Education Lender/Student Loan framework. Cited in the legislative session for context only.
- **SB 23-057 (2023)** — Title 30, Article 1 (County Treasurer fees). **Not a mortgage law** (amends §30-1-102 re: drainage/irrigation district treasurers). Cited here only because the legislative-number system is shared; this is a frequent source of confusion.
- **Title 12, Article 100 — "Colorado Mortgage Lending Act"** — this is the *current* umbrella mortgage-lending statute as reorganized in the 2020–2022 sunset review of the Division of Real Estate. DORA's sunset review (https://dora.colorado.gov/) confirms the Division of Real Estate is the mortgage licensing regulator. The current Article 100 structure (as of the 2024 Code of Colorado Regulations) consolidates the prior Article 61/61.5 framework and is the citation that should be used in modern NMLS filings, contracts, and disclosures.
- **4 CCR 725-1** is the **Division of Real Estate's** regulation chapter. (The search-fetched sample at `https://www.sos.state.co.us/CCR/GenerateRulePdf.do?ruleVersionId=11835&fileName=4%20CCR%20725-1` returns the Division of Insurance property/liability mass-marketing rule, **3 CCR 702-5**, illustrating a common Secretary of State PDF URL confusion. The actual current DRE mortgage rule is at the Secretary of State CCR portal under 4 CCR 725-1 — to be re-validated against the live Secretary of State portal before reliance.) For advertising-specific rules, the *operative* DRE advertising rules are now embedded in the consolidated rule chapter; the specific equal-housing-lender-logo, NMLS-unique-identifier, and rate/APR advertising rules are administered by DRE and enforced under the Consumer Protection Act and the Colorado Anti-Discrimination Act (CADA).

**NMLS unique identifier (federal + state):** Under 12 U.S.C. §5103 and 12 C.F.R. §1008 (SAFE Act), every Colorado-licensed MLO must obtain a unique identifier issued by the Nationwide Mortgage Licensing System and Registry (NMLS). DRE's NMLS process for Colorado is documented at https://dre.colorado.gov/mortgage-loan-originator. The Colorado-specific NMLS landing page is at https://mortgage.nmlsconsumeraccess.org/.

**Mortgage broker vs. mortgage lender (Colorado):**
- A **mortgage broker** solicits or places loans for borrowers *for compensation* but does not fund loans.
- A **mortgage lender** *funds* (or table-funds) the loan.
- DRE-registered mortgage companies must designate a **qualified individual** (the "Qualifying Individual" / Operations Manager) responsible for supervision. Both brokers and lenders must register through NMLS (Form MU1, MU2, MU3, MU4) and Colorado uses the NMLS as the system of record for licensing.

**Pre-qualification tool rules (Colorado):** There is no Colorado-specific *statute* labelled "pre-qualification" — but the Colorado UDAAP regime (the Colorado Consumer Protection Act) reaches any tool that issues a representation about a consumer's likelihood of obtaining a mortgage, and ECOA Regulation B (12 C.F.R. Part 1002) applies because the diagnostic collects information used to evaluate creditworthiness. (See §1.4 below on CCPA enforcement.)

## 1.2 The Colorado Privacy Act (CPA) — Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-1301 et seq.

**Citation / primary source:** Senate Bill 21-190 (SB 21-190), "Concerning Additional Protection of Data Relating to Personal Privacy," enacted July 7, 2021, effective **July 1, 2023**, codified at **Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-1301 et seq.** (Part 13, Article 1, Title 6).
- Bill text PDF: https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2021a_190_signed.pdf
- Leg page: https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb21-190
- The CPA is enforced only by the Colorado Attorney General and Colorado district attorneys (no private right of action). Source: §6-1-1311 of the enacted bill text.

**Definitions (directly from SB 21-190 enacted text):**

- **"Personal data"** — "information that is linked or reasonably linkable to an identified or identifiable individual" (§6-1-1303(17)). "Does not include de-identified data or publicly available information" (§6-1-1303(17)(b)).
- **"Profiling"** — "any form of automated processing of personal data to evaluate, analyze, or predict personal aspects concerning an identified or identifiable individual's **economic situation, health, personal preferences, interests, reliability, behavior, location, or movements**" (§6-1-1303(20)). *This is the trigger term for mortgage qualification diagnostics. A tool that uses income, debts, or credit-related data to "evaluate" a consumer's economic situation is performing "profiling."*
- **"Sale"** — "the exchange of personal data for monetary or other valuable consideration by a controller to a third party" (§6-1-1303(23)(a)), with detailed carve-outs (to processors, to affiliates, in a merger, at the consumer's direction, etc.).
- **"Sensitive data"** — defined narrowly at §6-1-1303(24) as:
  1. "Personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin, religious beliefs, a mental or physical health condition or diagnosis, sex life or sexual orientation, or citizenship or citizenship status";
  2. "Genetic or biometric data that may be processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying an individual"; or
  3. "Personal data from a known child."
  - *Important correction to common third-party "compliance checklists":* **SSN, account numbers, credit-card numbers, and financial-account information are NOT included in the Colorado "sensitive data" definition.** They are regulated under GLBA / Safeguards Rule and FCRA, not the CPA. CPA sensitive-data triggers for a mortgage diagnostic are: (i) any racial/ethnic origin data the user may volunteer; (ii) any health data the user may volunteer; (iii) any genetic/biometric data collected; (iv) any data on a known child (e.g., a co-borrower or household member under 13).
- **"Targeted advertising"** — §6-1-1303(25) — displaying ads "selected based on personal data obtained or inferred over time from the consumer's activities across nonaffiliated websites, applications, or online services to predict consumer preferences or interests." The statute expressly *excludes* contextual advertising and first-party advertising.

**Consumer rights (§6-1-1306):**
1. **Right to opt out of:**
   - (A) targeted advertising;
   - (B) the sale of personal data; **or**
   - (C) **"profiling in furtherance of decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects concerning a consumer"** (§6-1-1306(1)(a)(I)(C)).
   - A mortgage qualification result that affects whether a consumer will be solicited for, offered, or processed for a mortgage is a "decision that produces legal or similarly significant effects." The CPA therefore *requires* the controller (the diagnostic operator) to permit consumers to opt out of the underlying profiling.
2. Right of access.
3. Right to correction.
4. Right to deletion.
5. Right to data portability.

**Universal Opt-Out Mechanism (UOOM):** §6-1-1306(1)(a)(IV) requires controllers that process personal data for targeted advertising or sale to allow opt-out via a user-selected UOOM "that meets the technical specifications established by the Attorney General pursuant to §6-1-1313." A controller may still obtain a consumer's consent that overrides the UOOM via a "clear and conspicuous" web-form opt-in (§6-1-1306(1)(a)(IV)(C)).

**Duties of controllers (relevant excerpts):**
- **Purpose specification:** "specify the purposes for which personal data is collected and processed" (§6-1-1308(1)(a)).
- **Data minimization:** "collect personal data that is adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes for which it is processed" (§6-1-1308(1)(b)).
- **No secondary use** without consent (§6-1-1308(1)(c)).
- **No unlawful discrimination** for exercising rights (§6-1-1308(1)(g)).
- **Data Protection Assessments** are required where processing "presents a heightened risk of harm to consumers," including "profiling … where the profiling presents a reasonably foreseeable risk of … unfair or deceptive treatment or financial, physical, or reputational harm" (§6-1-1309(2)(c)(IV)). **A mortgage qualification diagnostic operated with profiling logic is squarely within this requirement.**
- **Sensitive-data processing requires affirmative opt-in consent** (§6-1-1308(5)).

**Exemptions relevant to mortgage lead-gen (§6-1-1304):**
- §6-1-1304(1) — personal data governed by GLBA (e.g., NPI collected by a financial institution), HIPAA, the Fair Credit Reporting Act, etc. **The CPA does NOT exempt all mortgage data** — only data "governed by" GLBA. If the diagnostic collects data outside of a financial-institution's GLBA-scope use (e.g., a marketing-data platform collecting a consumer's name + income for routing, which is not "financial institution" activity under GLBA), the CPA still applies.
- §6-1-1304(6) — "Consumer credit reporting agencies" subject to the FCRA are exempt.

**Consequence for a consumer-facing mortgage qualification diagnostic:** The CPA **does apply** to the diagnostic, even though the underlying lending decision is GLBA-governed at the lender level. The diagnostic operator is the controller; the lender (when the lead is transmitted) becomes a separate controller; the data is *not* necessarily "governed by" GLBA at the diagnostic level. **Opt-out for profiling under §6-1-1306(1)(a)(I)(C) and a Data Protection Assessment under §6-1-1309(2) are the two most actionable compliance items.**

## 1.3 The Colorado AI Act — SB 24-205 (2024)

**Citation / primary source:** Senate Bill 24-205 (SB 24-205), "Concerning Consumer Protections in Interactions with Artificial Intelligence Systems," enacted **May 17, 2024**, codified at **Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-1701 et seq.** (Part 17, Article 1, Title 6). 
- Bill text PDF: https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/2024a_205_signed.pdf
- Leg page: https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-205
- Wikipedia overview (good summary; cross-check against bill text): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado_AI_Act

**Effective date (key compliance date):** Per §6-1-1702(3)(a) and §6-1-1703(4)(a), the obligations on developers and deployers "**on and after February 1, 2026**" become operative. (Wikipedia summary notes a "June 30, 2026" commencement date in the long-title block of the bill, but the substantive obligations are pegged to **February 1, 2026** throughout §§6-1-1702 and 6-1-1703.)

**Definitions (from §6-1-1701(3)):**

> "(3) 'CONSEQUENTIAL DECISION' MEANS A DECISION THAT HAS A MATERIAL LEGAL OR SIMILARLY SIGNIFICANT EFFECT ON THE PROVISION OR DENIAL TO ANY CONSUMER OF, OR THE COST OR TERMS OF:
> (a) EDUCATION ENROLLMENT OR AN EDUCATION OPPORTUNITY;
> (b) EMPLOYMENT OR AN EMPLOYMENT OPPORTUNITY;
> (c) **A FINANCIAL OR LENDING SERVICE**;
> (d) AN ESSENTIAL GOVERNMENT SERVICE;
> (e) HEALTH-CARE SERVICES;
> (f) HOUSING;
> (g) INSURANCE; OR
> (h) A LEGAL SERVICE."

A mortgage qualification diagnostic that influences whether a consumer is offered a mortgage (or the price/terms at which a consumer is offered a mortgage) is making a **consequential decision** in two categories simultaneously: **(c) a financial or lending service** and **(f) housing**. There is no plausible reading of the bill that exempts a mortgage qualification diagnostic from the AI Act if it is a "high-risk AI system."

**"High-risk AI system" (from §6-1-1701(9)):**

> "(a) 'HIGH-RISK ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM' MEANS ANY ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM THAT, WHEN DEPLOYED, MAKES, OR IS A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN MAKING, A CONSEQUENTIAL DECISION."

> "(b) 'HIGH-RISK ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM' DOES NOT INCLUDE: (I) AN ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM IF THE ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM IS INTENDED TO: (A) PERFORM A NARROW PROCEDURAL TASK; OR (B) DETECT DECISION-MAKING PATTERNS OR DEVIATIONS FROM PRIOR DECISION-MAKING PATTERNS AND IS NOT INTENDED TO REPLACE OR INFLUENCE A PREVIOUSLY COMPLETED HUMAN ASSESSMENT WITHOUT SUFFICIENT HUMAN REVIEW; OR (II) THE FOLLOWING TECHNOLOGIES, UNLESS THE TECHNOLOGIES, WHEN DEPLOYED, MAKE, OR ARE A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN MAKING, A CONSEQUENTIAL DECISION: (A) ANTI-FRAUD TECHNOLOGY THAT DOES NOT USE FACIAL RECOGNITION TECHNOLOGY; ... (R) TECHNOLOGY THAT COMMUNICATES WITH CONSUMERS IN NATURAL LANGUAGE FOR THE PURPOSE OF PROVIDING USERS WITH INFORMATION, MAKING REFERRALS OR RECOMMENDATIONS, AND ANSWERING QUESTIONS AND IS SUBJECT TO AN ACCEPTED USE POLICY THAT PROHIBITS GENERATING CONTENT THAT IS DISCRIMINATORY OR HARMFUL."

**Material compliance question for a mortgage qualification diagnostic:**
- A "pre-qualification" or "mortgage-readiness" tool that issues a pass/fail or score is, by definition, "mak[ing], or [being] a substantial factor in making, a consequential decision" within categories (c) and (f). It is therefore a **high-risk AI system** under the Act.
- The narrow exclusions in (I) and (II) are unlikely to cover a generative/explanatory AI mortgage diagnostic: the tool is not a "narrow procedural task" and is not a "spam- and robocall-filtering" or "web hosting" technology. The exclusion in (II)(r) — "TECHNOLOGY THAT COMMUNICATES WITH CONSUMERS IN NATURAL LANGUAGE … AND IS SUBJECT TO AN ACCEPTED USE POLICY THAT PROHIBITS GENERATING CONTENT THAT IS DISCRIMINATORY OR HARMFUL" — would only cover a Q&A tool that does not influence a consequential decision, which a mortgage qualification result does.

**"Deployer" duty (from §6-1-1703):** the diagnostic operator is a **deployer** of a high-risk AI system (whether they developed it or are deploying a third-party system). Key deployer obligations under §6-1-1703 (operative February 1, 2026):

- **§6-1-1703(2) — Risk management policy and program:** "implement and maintain a risk management policy and program … to govern the deployment of any high-risk artificial intelligence system … the policy must be reasonable considering the … 'Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework' published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology or Standard ISO/IEC 42001 …"
- **§6-1-1703(3) — Impact assessment:** "A DEPLOYER, OR A THIRD PARTY CONTRACTED BY THE DEPLOYER, SHALL … COMPLETE AN IMPACT ASSESSMENT" of the high-risk system for each deployment, with a non-exhaustive list of required content (purpose, use case, benefit, known risks, mitigation steps, metrics, safeguards).
- **§6-1-1703(4) — Pre-decision consumer notice:** "ON AND AFTER FEBRUARY 1, 2026, AND NO LATER THAN THE TIME THAT A DEPLOYER DEPLOYS A HIGH-RISK ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM TO MAKE, OR BE A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN MAKING, A CONSEQUENTIAL DECISION CONCERNING A CONSUMER, THE DEPLOYER SHALL:
  (I) NOTIFY THE CONSUMER THAT THE DEPLOYER HAS DEPLOYED A HIGH-RISK ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM TO MAKE, OR BE A SUBSTANTIAL FACTOR IN MAKING, A CONSEQUENTIAL DECISION BEFORE THE DECISION IS MADE;
  (II) PROVIDE TO THE CONSUMER A STATEMENT DISCLOSING THE PURPOSE OF THE HIGH-RISK ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM AND THE NATURE OF THE CONSEQUENTIAL DECISION; THE CONTACT INFORMATION FOR THE DEPLOYER; A DESCRIPTION, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE, OF THE HIGH-RISK ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEM; AND INSTRUCTIONS ON HOW TO ACCESS THE STATEMENT REQUIRED BY SUBSECTION (5)(a) OF THIS SECTION; AND
  (III) PROVIDE TO THE CONSUMER INFORMATION, IF APPLICABLE, REGARDING THE CONSUMER'S RIGHT TO OPT OUT OF THE PROCESSING OF PERSONAL DATA CONCERNING THE CONSUMER FOR PURPOSES OF PROFILING IN FURTHERANCE OF DECISIONS THAT PRODUCE LEGAL OR SIMILARLY SIGNIFICANT EFFECTS CONCERNING THE CONSUMER UNDER SECTION 6-1-1306 (1)(a)(I)(C)."
- **§6-1-1703(4)(b) — Adverse-decision disclosures:** When the consequential decision is adverse, the deployer must disclose the principal reason, the degree and manner of AI contribution, the type and source of data processed, an opportunity to correct incorrect personal data, and an opportunity to appeal that "if technically feasible, allow[s] for human review." All notices must be in plain language, in any language in which the deployer ordinarily provides contracts, in a format accessible to consumers with disabilities, and provided directly to the consumer.
- **§6-1-1703(5) — Public statement on the deployer's website:** "A DEPLOYER SHALL MAKE AVAILABLE, IN A MANNER THAT IS CLEAR AND READILY AVAILABLE ON THE DEPLOYER'S WEBSITE, A STATEMENT SUMMARIZING: (I) THE TYPES OF HIGH-RISK ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE SYSTEMS THAT ARE CURRENTLY DEPLOYED BY THE DEPLOYER; (II) HOW THE DEPLOYER MANAGES KNOWN OR REASONABLY FORESEEABLE RISKS OF ALGORITHMIC DISCRIMINATION …; AND (III) IN DETAIL, THE NATURE, SOURCE, AND EXTENT OF THE INFORMATION COLLECTED AND USED BY THE DEPLOYER."

**"Developer" duty (from §6-1-1702):** if the diagnostic operator *also develops* the AI system, §6-1-1702 imposes additional documentation, public-statement, and 90-day disclosure-to-AG obligations. Where the operator merely uses a third-party AI (e.g., a model API), the operator is a deployer and the developer is upstream. The deployer must obtain the developer's statement and documentation (§6-1-1703(2)).

**Small-business exception (§6-1-1703(6)):** A deployer that (a) employs fewer than 50 full-time-equivalent employees *and* (b) does not use its own data to train the high-risk system is exempt from the risk-management, impact-assessment, and public-statement requirements under §§6-1-1703(2), (3), and (5). The pre-decision consumer notice (§6-1-1703(4)) is **not** excused. *If the operator is a small business relying on a third-party AI system, it still must provide the pre-decision notice and the consumer-opt-out information.*

**Enforcement (§6-1-1706 / §6-1-1701 of the Consumer Protection Act):** enforced only by the Colorado Attorney General. Maximum civil penalty of **$20,000 per violation** under the Colorado Consumer Protection Act, §6-1-112(1).

**Federal preemption / banking exemption (§6-1-1701(8)):** banks, credit unions, and their affiliates "subject to examination by a state or federal prudential regulator" are in full compliance with the Act if the federal regulator's published guidance on AI is substantially equivalent. A lead-generation website that is not itself a bank or credit union does not benefit from this safe harbor. The mortgage lender that ultimately receives the lead may benefit, but the diagnostic itself must comply.

**Application to a mortgage qualification diagnostic:** The diagnostic's AI system is high-risk. The deployer (operator) must (i) have a written risk-management policy; (ii) have completed a pre-deployment impact assessment; (iii) provide the pre-decision notice to the consumer; (iv) provide the adverse-decision notice with AI contribution explanation; (v) provide an appeal/correction process; (vi) post the public website statement; and (vii) keep records subject to AG inspection.

## 1.4 Colorado Consumer Protection Act — §6-1-101 et seq.

**Citation / primary source:** Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-101 et seq. ("Colorado Consumer Protection Act" or CCPA — to be distinguished from the California Consumer Privacy Act, which is the "CCPA" most commonly abbreviated that way in 2024+ literature). The Act is enforced by the Colorado Attorney General and, after AG/District Attorney notice, by private litigants (with limited remedies; no treble damages; attorneys' fees only in limited circumstances — see §6-1-113).
- AG portal: https://coag.gov/

**Application to mortgage lead-gen:** The Colorado AG has repeatedly taken action against mortgage companies, debt-relief services, and student-loan servicers under §§6-1-101 and 6-1-105 for deceptive trade practices. The Act declares specific per se deceptive trade practices; a mortgage qualification diagnostic that represents a "pre-qualification" or "pre-approval" without licensing, that fails to disclose lender identity, that misrepresents approval likelihood, or that sells leads in violation of federal and state privacy law is at material risk.

**Recent enforcement (2023–2024) relevant to mortgage / lead-gen / AI:** The CO AG has been active in enforcing the new Colorado Privacy Act (which provides that "a violation of its requirements is a deceptive trade practice for purposes of enforcement" under SB 21-190 final text) and has investigated and settled with several data-brokers. To obtain the current list of formal enforcement actions, query the AG's press release archive at https://coag.gov/ (search "mortgage," "lead," "privacy," and "AI"). The author of this report has not independently revalidated the specific 2023–2024 settlement docket; reverify before relying on any case-specific citation.

- Consumer Protection landing: https://coag.gov/ (resource pages change — use the AG site search for "mortgage enforcement" or "data privacy enforcement" to find current press releases).

## 1.5 Colorado mortgage advertising rules

**Statutory:** the operative advertising rules are:
- **Colo. Rev. Stat. Title 12, Article 100 (current)** — the consolidated mortgage lending statute, including MLO identification and unique-identifier requirements, and prohibitions on false, misleading, or deceptive advertising by mortgage brokers and lenders. To be revalidated against the live DORA sunset report and current CRS for the exact section numbers.
- **Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-101 et seq. (Colorado Consumer Protection Act)** — applies to any "trade or commerce" — i.e., to a mortgage lead-generation website — and reaches deceptive trade practices in advertising.
- **Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-1301 et seq. (Colorado Privacy Act)** — adds per se CPA violations for privacy violations.
- **Colorado Anti-Discrimination Act (CADA) — Colo. Rev. Stat. §24-34-101 et seq.** — for any housing-related advertising, includes the FHA "Equal Housing Opportunity" logo requirement at 24 C.F.R. Part 110 (federal) and the CADA's housing-provisions analogue.

**Regulation (DRE):** the Division of Real Estate's advertising rules are in the consolidated 4 CCR 725-1 (or successor chapter after the 2020–2024 rule consolidation). The exact section numbers (e.g., the version of the rule that mirrors the NMLS consumer-access web page requirement, the rule that prohibits government-emblem use, the rule that requires the MLO's name and unique identifier adjacent to any rate quote) must be revalidated against the live Secretary of State CCR portal before reliance.

**Common advertising rules that recur in every state (and therefore in Colorado):**
- Display of **NMLS unique identifier** in close proximity to any rate, term, or product reference (12 U.S.C. §5103 / 12 C.F.R. §1008).
- Display of **Equal Housing Opportunity** logo (24 C.F.R. Part 110).
- **No government-emblems** (e.g., "official-looking" seals) on consumer-facing material.
- No "guaranteed approval" or "guaranteed lowest rate" claims unless literally true.
- No use of the word "free" in a way that is deceptive (cross-reference FTC 16 C.F.R. §251.1, which Colorado regulators routinely apply by analogy under the CCPA).

## 1.7 What is unique about Colorado

Colorado is the **first U.S. state to enact a comprehensive, state-level "high-risk AI" law** that:
1. Brings a "consequential decision" framework into state consumer-protection law, expressly including **financial/lending services** and **housing**.
2. Imposes *both* developer *and* deployer duties (a 2024 redesign from the original SB 24-205 framework).
3. Is pegged to a **February 1, 2026** operative date, with **2025 the practical compliance runway** for products that are already in market.

Colorado is also one of a small number of states with:
- A **comprehensive consumer privacy law** (the CPA) that *expressly* creates a profiling-opt-out right tied to "decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects" — a mortgage qualification diagnostic is squarely within the operative language of §6-1-1306(1)(a)(I)(C).
- An **AG-only** enforcement regime for the CPA and the AI Act (no private right of action for the consumer).

## 1.8 Sample safe disclosure language — Colorado

The following templates are illustrative. **They are not a substitute for review by Colorado-licensed mortgage compliance counsel.**

> **1. Pre-decision AI notice (Colorado AI Act, §6-1-1703(4)(a))** — to be shown *before* the diagnostic returns a result:
>
> *"This site uses an artificial-intelligence system to help evaluate whether a mortgage product may be available to you. The system is a 'high-risk AI system' under the Colorado AI Act (Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-1701 et seq.). The decision it makes — or substantially contributes to — is a 'consequential decision' affecting your access to a financial or lending service and to housing. The Company is the deployer. The AI is operated by [Company] with oversight by a qualified human under our risk-management policy. You can request (a) a description of the AI system, (b) the principal reason for any adverse result, (c) the type and source of data we process, (d) the opportunity to correct inaccurate personal data, and (e) the opportunity to appeal any adverse decision with human review. Contact us at [email] or [telephone] for any of these requests. You may also opt out of profiling in furtherance of consequential decisions under the Colorado Privacy Act (Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-1306(1)(a)(I)(C)) — see our Do Not Sell or Profile My Information page."*

> **2. CPA opt-out footer (Colorado Privacy Act, §6-1-1306(1)(a)(I))** — to appear in a "Do Not Sell or Profile" page:
>
> *"Colorado residents have the right to opt out of (a) targeted advertising, (b) the sale of personal data, and (c) profiling in furtherance of decisions that produce legal or similarly significant effects concerning them. To exercise this right, [click here] or use a user-selected Universal Opt-Out Mechanism such as the Global Privacy Control. To authorize another person to act on your behalf, follow the instructions at [URL]. We will not discriminate against you for exercising these rights."*

> **3. Required company/license/NMLS disclosures (DRE)** — to appear on every public-facing page of the website (or at least on the page where any rate or term is mentioned):
>
> *"Equal Housing Opportunity. [Company Name] is a [mortgage broker / mortgage lender] licensed by the Colorado Division of Real Estate. NMLS unique identifier #[______]. NMLS Consumer Access: https://www.nmlsconsumeraccess.org/. This is not a commitment to lend. Not all applicants will qualify. Rates, terms, and availability of products are subject to change without notice. The pre-qualification results shown on this page are not an application for credit and do not guarantee approval for any mortgage product. All information collected is subject to our Privacy Policy and the Colorado Privacy Act (Colo. Rev. Stat. §6-1-1301 et seq.)."*

> **4. DRE / SAFE Act / state-license disclosure** (mirrors NMLS consumer-access requirements):
>
> *"Loans are arranged by [Company Name], NMLS #[______]. [Company Name] is licensed by the Colorado Division of Real Estate (Colorado Mortgage Lender / Broker License #[______]). [Company Name] does not provide tax, legal, or financial advice. Information you provide is used to provide a preliminary, educational estimate of the mortgage products for which you may qualify. This estimate is not a loan application, a loan commitment, a loan guarantee, or pre-approval."*

> **5. Adverse-decision / right-to-correction / appeal (Colorado AI Act, §6-1-1703(4)(b))** — to be shown with every "you likely won't qualify" result:
>
> *"The AI system's principal reason for this adverse result is: [specific factor]. The AI's contribution to the result: [plain-language description]. The categories of data processed: [income, debts, equity, location, etc.]. The sources of that data: [the inputs you provided; the credit-bureau partner; the public records; etc.]. You may (a) correct any inaccurate personal data, (b) submit additional information, and (c) appeal this decision for human review. To start an appeal, go to [URL] or call [telephone] within 30 days. The appeal will be reviewed by a qualified human reviewer who was not the AI system."*

> **6. Data Protection Assessment disclosure (Colorado Privacy Act, §6-1-1309(2))** — for the diagnostic operator's risk-management file:
>
> *"A Data Protection Assessment for the mortgage qualification AI was last completed on [date] and is on file with the Company. The assessment addresses the heightened risk of harm arising from profiling in furtherance of consequential decisions, including the reasonably foreseeable risk of unfair treatment. A copy of the assessment is available to the Colorado Attorney General upon request."*

> **7. Notice for text / SMS lead follow-up** (general, not state-specific, but Colorado CPA reaches it if consumer is Colorado-resident):
>
> *"You will receive a follow-up text from [Company] about mortgage products that may be available to you. Message and data rates may apply. You can opt out at any time by replying STOP. By submitting your information, you consent to receive automated calls and texts from [Company] and its licensed marketing partners at the number you provided, including a wireless number, even if it is on any state or federal Do-Not-Call list. Consent is not a condition of purchase."*

> **8. Geofencing / My Health My Data / age gating** — Colorado's CPA requires affirmative opt-in for sensitive data. The diagnostic should:
> - Refuse to collect voluntary disclosures of racial or ethnic origin, religion, mental/physical health, sexual orientation, citizenship status, genetic data, or biometric data.
> - Not target advertising to consumers under 13 (the CPA's "known child" definition).
> - **Not** use geofencing around health-care facilities (the practice is regulated by Washington RCW 19.373 — see §6.5 — and is increasingly enforced under state CPAs; an interstate mortgage diagnostic should not implement geofencing around any health-care facility).

## 2.1 Georgia Department of Banking and Finance (DBF) and the GRMA

**Lead regulator:** the Georgia **Department of Banking and Finance (DBF)**, specifically the **Non-Depository Financial Institutions Division**, supervises Georgia-licensed or registered mortgage brokers, mortgage lenders, and mortgage loan originators. **Statutory framework:** the **Georgia Residential Mortgage Act (GRMA)**, codified at **O.C.G.A. Title 7, Chapter 1, Article 13**. Source: DBF website description — "Laws governing the residential mortgage industry are primarily found in Title 7, Chapter 1, Article 13 of the Official Code of Georgia Annotated (O.C.G.A.)." (https://dbf.georgia.gov/mb-brokers-lenders-and-originators/mortgage-laws-and-rules). **Mortgage broker vs. mortgage lender (Georgia):** under the GRMA, a **mortgage broker** is a person who for compensation directly or indirectly negotiates, places, or finds mortgage loans for others; a **mortgage lender** is a person who makes mortgage loans or services mortgage loans for others, or who directly or indirectly sells or offers to sell a mortgage loan to a non-institutional investor. **Pre-qualification tool rules (Georgia):** the GRMA does not have a specifically named "pre-qualification" sub-regime. The operative compliance regime is:
- **GRMA licensing trigger:** any person performing loan-origination activities, including taking applications, must be licensed. (Specific O.C.G.A. §7-1-1000 et seq. section numbers to be revalidated against the official O.C.G.A. text.)
- **UDAAP/FBPA** (§10-1-390 et seq., see §2.3) and **Fair Lending Act** (§7-6A-1 et seq., see §2.2) for any representation to a consumer about likelihood of qualifying.
- **SAFE Act** at the federal level for MLO conduct.

## 2.2 Georgia Fair Lending Act — O.C.G.A. §7-6A-1 et seq.

**Citation:** the Georgia Fair Lending Act is at **O.C.G.A. Title 7, Chapter 6A**, sections 7-6A-1 through 7-6A-20 (approximately; exact section range to be revalidated against the live O.C.G.A.). The Act mirrors the federal ECOA (15 U.S.C. §1691 et seq.) and Regulation B (12 C.F.R. Part 1002) in important respects, with Georgia-specific differences including:
- Application to non-depository mortgage brokers and lenders (not just banks).
- A state-level prohibition on lending discrimination that includes the federal protected classes and adds **sexual orientation** under O.C.G.A. §7-6A-1 (added by amendment in 2020 — to be revalidated against the live statute). (The AG has, at various times, asserted that discrimination based on sexual orientation in mortgage lending is also reached under Georgia's state-level fair-lending authority.)
- State-level enforcement by the Georgia AG.

**Application to a mortgage qualification diagnostic:** the diagnostic evaluates "creditworthiness" using a consumer's financial information. If the diagnostic uses a protected characteristic or a proxy for one (e.g., ZIP code, race, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, familial status, handicap, or sexual orientation), the diagnostic is potentially engaged in a "fair lending" practice. A diagnostic that produces disparate results across protected classes is at material risk of a state-level investigation by the AG, parallel to a CFPB / HUD / DOJ investigation under ECOA / FHA.

## 2.4 Georgia Telephone Solicitation / mini-TCPA — O.C.G.A. §46-5-27, HB 590 (2019)

**Citation / primary source:** the **Georgia Telephone Solicitation Act of 2019** was enacted as **HB 590** (2019 Regular Session) and codified at **O.C.G.A. §46-5-27** ("Telephone solicitation"). The Act was further amended by **HB 637 (2022)** to address prerecorded messages and to add private rights of action. Both amendments and the current text are available through the O.C.G.A. portal. (Specific section numbers — §46-5-27(a), (b), (c), (d) — to be revalidated against the live O.C.G.A.)

Key provisions (as enacted and amended):
- **Prohibits telemarketing calls** to numbers on the National Do-Not-Call Registry.
- **Time-of-day restrictions** (commonly 9 a.m. – 9 p.m. local time at the called party's location).
- **Caller-ID / spoofing** prohibitions.
- **Required disclosures** at the start of a telemarketing call: (i) the identity of the seller; (ii) that the purpose is a sales call; (iii) the nature of the goods or services.
- **Opt-out** at any time during the call.
- **Record-keeping** of consent, opt-out, and DNC compliance.
- **SMS / text** provisions are not as comprehensively addressed in §46-5-27 as in some other states; the federal **TCPA** (47 U.S.C. §227) and **FCC rules** (47 C.F.R. §64.1200) govern SMS / text in Georgia absent a state-level mini-TCPA amendment.

**Application to a mortgage qualification diagnostic:** any outbound call or text to a Georgia consumer as part of lead follow-up must comply with §46-5-27 (calls), the federal TCPA (calls and texts), and the FCC's prior-express-written-consent rule for prerecorded or autodialed marketing messages. A mortgage lead-gen site that triggers a callback via a "form-fill" must capture a TCPA-compliant consent disclosure (clear and conspicuous, separate from any other agreement, acknowledging that consent is not a condition of purchase) and must honor opt-outs.

## 2.6 What is unique about Georgia

- The GRMA is a **fee-remitting act** — Georgia-licensed and registered mortgage lenders/brokers must remit a percentage of the loan to the State of Georgia (the "GRMA fee") on closed Georgia residential mortgage loans. A lead-generation website that operates through a Georgia-licensed broker must ensure that the broker remains current on GRMA remittance.
- Georgia's mini-TCPA is **notably narrower** than other state mini-TCPAs (Florida, Oklahoma, Washington, etc.) — Georgia has not yet enacted a comprehensive text-message opt-in regime at the state level; the federal TCPA dominates the regulatory landscape.
- Georgia **does not have a comprehensive state consumer-privacy law** comparable to the Colorado Privacy Act, the California CCPA/CPRA, the Connecticut CTDPA, etc. (Georgia has a data-breach notification law, O.C.G.A. §10-1-910 et seq., but no general consumer-privacy statute as of 2024–2025 — to be revalidated against the current legislative session).
- Georgia **does not have a state AI Act** comparable to Colorado's SB 24-205 as of 2024–2025. The Georgia AG's office has issued guidance on AI and on algorithmic discrimination in employment and housing, but the state's enforcement framework for AI in mortgage qualification derives from the Fair Business Practices Act and the Fair Lending Act.

# 3. OHIO

## 3.1 Ohio Department of Commerce — Division of Financial Institutions (DFI) and the RMLA

**Lead regulator:** the **Ohio Department of Commerce, Division of Financial Institutions (DFI)** supervises Ohio-licensed mortgage brokers, mortgage lenders, mortgage servicers, and mortgage loan originators under the **Ohio Residential Mortgage Lending Act (RMLA)**, codified at **Ohio Rev. Code Chapter 1322**.
- DFI home: https://www.com.ohio.gov/divisions/fininst
- DFI consolidated URL: https://www.com.ohio.gov/divisions-and-offices/financial-institutions
- Ohio Revised Code portal: https://codes.ohio.gov/

**Primary statute — Ohio Rev. Code Chapter 1322 (RMLA):** the chapter is divided into (among other things):
- **R.C. §1322.01 — Definitions** (effective September 1, 2021, last amended by House Bill 133, 134th General Assembly). Key definitions include:
  - **(B) "Advertising"** — *"A COMMERCIAL MESSAGE IN ANY MEDIUM THAT PROMOTES, EITHER DIRECTLY OR INDIRECTLY, A RESIDENTIAL MORTGAGE LENDING TRANSACTION."* This is the operative definition for any consumer-facing mortgage qualification diagnostic that promotes a lending transaction.
  - **(Y) "Mortgage broker"** — *"AN ENTITY THAT FOR COMPENSATION OR GAIN, OR IN THE EXPECTATION OF COMPENSATION OR GAIN, OBTAINS, ATTEMPTS TO OBTAIN, OR ASSISTS IN OBTAINING A RESIDENTIAL MORTGAGE LOAN FOR A BORROWER FROM A MORTGAGE LENDER. FOR PURPOSES OF THIS DIVISION, 'ATTEMPTING TO OBTAIN OR ASSISTING IN OBTAINING' A RESIDENTIAL MORTGAGE LOAN INCLUDES REFERRING A BORROWER TO A MORTGAGE LENDER, SOLICITING OR OFFERING TO SOLICIT A MORTGAGE LOAN ON BEHALF OF A BORROWER, OR NEGOTIATING OR OFFERING TO NEGOTIATE THE TERMS OR CONDITIONS OF A MORTGAGE LOAN WITH A MORTGAGE LENDER ON BEHALF OF A BORROWER."* **This is the critical definition for a lead-generation website.** A site that takes a borrower's information and routes it to a lender is "assisting in obtaining" a loan. A site that displays rates or terms sourced from a lender is "promoting" a mortgage lending transaction.
  - **(Z) "Mortgage lender"** — *"AN ENTITY THAT FOR COMPENSATION OR GAIN, OR IN THE EXPECTATION OF COMPENSATION OR GAIN CONSUMMATES A RESIDENTIAL MORTGAGE LOAN, ADVANCES FUNDS, OFFERS TO ADVANCE FUNDS, OR COMMITS TO ADVANCING FUNDS FOR A BORROWER."*
  - **(AA) "Mortgage loan originator"** — includes any individual who (a) takes a residential mortgage loan application, (b) assists or offers to assist a buyer in obtaining or applying to obtain a residential mortgage loan, (c) offers or negotiates terms, (d) issues or offers to issue a commitment. A diagnostic that gives the consumer concrete next-step instructions that an MLO would otherwise provide (e.g., "based on your results, our loan officer will call you in 24 hours") risks being treated as performing MLO activities if not performed by a licensed MLO.
  - **(II) "Residential mortgage loan"** — a loan primarily for personal, family, or household use secured by a mortgage on a dwelling or on residential real estate.
  - **(MM) "Unique identifier"** — *"A NUMBER OR OTHER IDENTIFIER ASSIGNED BY PROTOCOLS ESTABLISHED BY THE NATIONWIDE MORTGAGE LICENSING SYSTEM AND REGISTRY."* (i.e., the NMLS unique identifier.)
  - **(LL) "Transaction of business as a mortgage lender, mortgage servicer, or mortgage broker in this state"** — *"ORIGINATING, BROKERING, OR SERVICING FIVE OR MORE RESIDENTIAL MORTGAGE LOANS IN ANY TWELVE-MONTH PERIOD"* in any of three specified circumstances (any Ohio resident; any Ohio property; any person physically located in Ohio).
- **R.C. §1322.02 — Applicability** (exemptions include banks, savings banks, credit unions, bona fide nonprofit organizations, depository institutions that are exempt under federal banking law, etc.).
- **R.C. §1322.05** (registration certificate requirements), **R.C. §1322.07** (no person shall engage in the transaction of business as a mortgage lender, mortgage servicer, or mortgage broker in Ohio without first having obtained a certificate of registration from the superintendent of financial institutions for the principal office and every branch office); R.C. §1322.10 (conditions for issuing the certificate; financial responsibility, experience, general fitness, no material misstatements, no disqualifying offenses, no prior revocation, surety bond per §1322.32).
- **R.C. §1322.20** (MLO license), **R.C. §1322.28** (continuing education — 8 hours/year, including 3 hours federal law/reg, 2 hours ethics/fair lending, 2 hours non-QM/nontraditional lending), **R.C. §1322.30** (interest cap: a registrant or letter-of-exemption holder may contract for and receive interest at any rate agreed upon by the parties, but **not exceeding an annual percentage rate of 25 percent**).
- **R.C. §1322.41 — Prohibited interest in appraisal company** (a registrant, or immediate family of an owner, may not own or control a majority interest in an appraisal company).
- **R.C. §1322.42 — Supervision; remote work.** Subsection (C) addresses remote work by an MLO or associated person, subject to (a) a written supervision policy, (b) information-security controls, (c) no customer interactions at the employee's residence (unless it is a licensed principal/branch office), and (d) no physical records at the remote location. The superintendent may charge an annual fee up to $25 per remote location. (Effective September 13, 2022; SB 264, 134th General Assembly.)
- **R.C. §1322.43 — Disclosures in advertising.** See §3.4 below.
- **R.C. §1322.45 — Prohibited acts** (perjury, misrepresentation, fraud, willful violation of state or federal law, etc.).
- **R.C. §1322.46 — Disclosures in advertising.** See §3.4 below.
- **R.C. §1322.50 — Penalties** (revocation, suspension, fine, restitution, cease-and-desist).
- **R.C. §1322.55 — SAFE Act conformity rule** (the superintendent may adopt, by rule, provisions substantially equivalent to or more stringent than the SAFE Act, with limited rule-making procedure and an 18-month sunset for each rule).
- **R.C. §1322.57 — Administrative rules.**
- **R.C. §1322.99 — Penalties** (misdemeanor / felony classifications for certain violations).

**NMLS unique identifier:** required for every Ohio-licensed MLO. Ohio uses the NMLS as the system of record for state licensing.

**Ohio Administrative Code (OAC) — Chapter 1301:5-1 (mortgage lending rules):** the DFI's administrative rules implementing Chapter 1322. The current OAC chapter is to be revalidated against the live Ohio Administrative Code portal. The OAC 1301:5-1 rules include licensing fees, examinations, advertising supplements, branch-office standards, and recordkeeping.

**Mortgage broker vs. mortgage lender (Ohio):** as defined in R.C. §1322.01(Y) and (Z) above. Both require a certificate of registration; both must use NMLS.

## 3.4 Ohio mortgage advertising rules — R.C. §1322.46 and OAC 1301:5-1

## 3.6 What is unique about Ohio

- **Interest-rate cap:** R.C. §1322.30 caps residential mortgage loan interest at 25% APR for non-bank registrants. While this is a cap, not a per se prohibition, it is a hard cap that is rarely seen in other states.
- **25% APR cap** (R.C. §1322.30) is a salient compliance issue if the lead-generation site ever displays rates for subprime products; the displayed rate cannot exceed 25% APR unless the lender is exempt.
- **"Assisting in obtaining" — broad definition of "mortgage broker"** (R.C. §1322.01(Y)): the definition expressly includes "referring a borrower to a mortgage lender, soliciting or offering to solicit a mortgage loan on behalf of a borrower, or negotiating or offering to negotiate the terms or conditions of a mortgage loan with a mortgage lender on behalf of a borrower." A lead-generation site that performs *any* of these activities — even a "qualification routing" function — is potentially a mortgage broker in Ohio.
- **Five-loan-in-12-months trigger** (R.C. §1322.01(LL)): a person is "transacting business as a mortgage lender, mortgage servicer, or mortgage broker" in Ohio if they originate, broker, or service **five or more** residential mortgage loans in any 12-month period in any of three specified circumstances (any Ohio resident; any Ohio property; any person physically located in Ohio). This is a low-volume trigger.
- **"Five-loan" trigger is the licensing floor**, not a per se safe harbor: even one loan may be sufficient to require licensing if other factors (compensation, expectation of compensation, etc.) are present. The five-loan provision is an *additional* bright-line in R.C. §1322.01(LL).
- **Mandatory "registrant and unique-identifier in every advertisement" rule** (R.C. §1322.46) is the explicit Ohio statutory source of the federal NMLS Consumer Access rule, and is broader than federal requirements in that it applies to "any printed, televised, broadcast, electronically transmitted, or published advertisement … including on any electronic site accessible through the internet."

## 4.1 DIFS and the Mortgage Brokers, Lenders, and Servicers Licensing Act — MCL §445.1651 et seq.

**Lead regulator:** the **Michigan Department of Insurance and Financial Services (DIFS)**, which since the 2015 merger combines the former Office of Financial and Insurance Regulation (OFIR) and the former Office of Insurance. DIFS is the state mortgage regulator.

**Statute — Act 173 of 1987, the "Mortgage Brokers, Lenders, and Servicers Licensing Act"** (codified at MCL §445.1651 et seq.):
- **MCL §445.1651 — Short title:** *"THIS ACT SHALL BE KNOWN AND MAY BE CITED AS THE 'MORTGAGE BROKERS, LENDERS, AND SERVICERS LICENSING ACT'."*
- **MCL §445.1652 — License required.** *"A PERSON SHALL NOT ACT AS A MORTGAGE BROKER, MORTGAGE LENDER, OR MORTGAGE SERVICER WITHOUT FIRST OBTAINING A LICENSE UNDER THIS ACT OR REGISTERING UNDER SECTION 6, UNLESS 1 OR MORE OF THE FOLLOWING APPLY …"* (Subsections (a)–(d) list exemptions, including (a) loan officer services as an employee or agent of only one mortgage broker, mortgage lender, or mortgage servicer and a licensed loan officer if that licensure is required under the mortgage loan originator licensing act, (b) persons exempted under section 25, (c) class I licensees under the consumer financial services act, (d) employees of professional employer organizations acting as residential mortgage originators of only one broker or lender subject to the listed direct-and-control requirements.)
  - **Subsection (3):** *"A LOAN OFFICER SHALL NOT DIRECTLY OR INDIRECTLY RECEIVE ANY COMPENSATION, COMMISSION, FEE, POINTS, OR OTHER REMUNERATION OR BENEFITS FOR ORIGINATING A MORTGAGE LOAN UNLESS BOTH OF THE FOLLOWING ARE MET: (a) THE LOAN OFFICER IS A LICENSED LOAN OFFICER. (b) THE COMPENSATION, COMMISSION, FEE, POINTS, OR OTHER REMUNERATION OR BENEFITS ARE PAID BY THE LICENSEE OR REGISTRANT FOR WHICH THE LOAN OFFICER ORIGINATED THAT MORTGAGE LOAN."*
  - **Subsection (4):** a mortgage broker, lender, or servicer shall not pay any compensation to (a) a loan officer who is not a licensed loan officer, or (b) a licensed loan officer who is not an employee or agent of that broker, lender, or servicer.
- **MCL §445.1653 — Application.** Application to the commissioner on a form prescribed; investigation; license to be issued if the experience, character, business reputation, and general fitness of the applicant and its officers, directors, shareholders, partners, and affiliates "command the confidence of the public and warrant the belief that the applicant … will comply with the law."
- **MCL §445.1671 — Books and records; reports.** Licensee or registrant must maintain books, accounts, records, and documents of the business, as prescribed by the commissioner. Mortgage loan documents preserved for the longer of (i) transfer/assignment, (ii) **3 years after the date the mortgage loan is closed**. (Specific preservation rules for transferred/assigned loans and rejected applications; specific 3-year rule for other books and records after the conclusion of the fiscal year of creation.)
- **MCL §445.1672 — Prohibited acts.** It is a violation for a licensee or registrant to:
  - (a) Fail to conduct the business in accordance with law, this act, or a rule promulgated or order issued under this act.
  - (b) **Engage in fraud, deceit, or material misrepresentation in connection with any transaction governed by this act.**
  - (c) **Intentionally or due to gross or wanton negligence, repeatedly fail to provide borrowers material disclosures of information as required by law.**
  - (d) Suppress or withhold from the commissioner any information that the licensee or registrant possesses and that, if submitted, would have made the licensee or registrant ineligible for licensing or registration.
  - (e) Fail to comply with 1966 PA 125, MCL §565.161 to §565.164 (mortgage escrow account rules).
  - (f) Until proper disbursement is made, fail to place in a trust or escrow account held by a federally insured depository financial institution in a manner approved by the commissioner any money, funds, deposits, checks, drafts, or other negotiable instruments received by the licensee that the borrower is obligated to pay to a third party, including amounts paid to the holder of the mortgage loan, amounts for property taxes and insurance premiums, or amounts paid under an agreement that requires if the mortgage loan is not closed the amounts paid shall be refunded to the prospective borrower or if the mortgage loan is closed the amounts paid shall be applied to fees and costs incurred at the time the mortgage loan is closed. Fees and costs include title insurance premiums and recording fees; **fees and costs do not include amounts paid to cover costs incurred to process the mortgage loan application, to obtain an appraisal, or to receive a credit report.**
  - (g) Refuse to permit an examination or investigation.
  - (h) **To be convicted of a felony, or any misdemeanor of which an essential element is fraud.**
  - (i) Refuse or fail to pay, within a reasonable time, those expenses assessed.
  - (j) Fail to make restitution after having been ordered to do so.
  - (additional subsections to be revalidated against the live MCL)
- **MCL §445.1674 — Annual statement to borrower (mortgage servicer).** A mortgage servicer shall deliver to the borrower annually a statement showing the unpaid principal balance, the interest paid during the 12-month period, and the amounts deposited into escrow and disbursed from escrow. Within 25 days after receipt of a written request, a mortgage servicer shall deliver a ledger history for the immediately preceding 12-month period. A fee may not be charged for one annual statement and one ledger history in any 12-month period.
- **MCL §445.1675 — Exemptions.** This act does not apply to:
  - (a) A depository financial institution.
  - (b) A salesperson acting as agent for a residential builder or residential maintenance and alteration contractor.
  - (c) A real estate broker or real estate salesperson who is not a mortgage broker, mortgage lender, or mortgage servicer, or who only acts as a mortgage broker in connection with a real estate sale or lease and acts without additional compensation beyond the customary commission.
  - (d) A real estate salesperson who acts for a real estate broker as a mortgage broker, mortgage lender, or mortgage servicer and who receives for the services compensation only from the real estate broker for which the salesperson is an agent or employee.
  - (e) A person licensed under the secondary mortgage loan act (1981 PA 125, MCL §493.51 to §493.81), not making, brokering, or servicing mortgage loans as described in this act in a 12-month period from January 1 to December 31.
  - (f) Agencies or corporate instrumentalities of the United States and of this state and its political subdivisions, including the public employees' retirement system.
  - (g) **A mortgage lender that in the aggregate with any affiliates makes 10 or fewer mortgage loans in a 12-month period from January 1 to December 31.**
  - (h) **A mortgage servicer that in the aggregate with any affiliates services 10 or fewer mortgage loans in a 12-month period from January 1 to December 31.**
  - (i) **A mortgage servicer that in the aggregate with any affiliates services only 75 or fewer land contracts, of which 10 or fewer require the collection of money for the payment of taxes or insurance.**

## 4.6 What is unique about Michigan

- **10-loan-in-12-months de minimis exemption (MCL §445.1675(g) and (h)):** a Michigan mortgage lender that, in the aggregate with any affiliates, makes 10 or fewer mortgage loans in a 12-month period (Jan 1 – Dec 31) is exempt from the MBLSA; a Michigan mortgage servicer that services 10 or fewer loans in a 12-month period is also exempt. This is a bright-line, low-volume safe harbor. **Note: this does *not* exempt the person from the Michigan Mortgage Loan Originator Licensing Act (the SAFE Act implementation) or from federal law (ECOA, Reg B, RESPA, Reg N, GLBA, FCRA, TCPA).**
- **Compensation-flow control (MCL §445.1652(3)–(4)):** loan officers must be licensed; compensation for originating a loan must flow from the licensee or registrant through the licensed loan officer. This is one of the most explicit "no payment to unlicensed loan officers" rules in the country.
- **10-loan-12-month-aggregate test** is computed "in the aggregate with any affiliates" — so a multi-entity lead-generation business cannot spread volume across affiliates to stay under the 10-loan cap.
- **"Mortgage servicer" definition includes collecting escrow for taxes and insurance (MCL §445.1675(i)):** the 10-loan and 75-land-contract exemptions have specific carve-outs for "money for the payment of taxes or insurance" — a small servicer that collects escrow must comply with the MBLSA's escrow rules (MCL §565.161 to §565.164) regardless of volume.
- **Michigan's No-Call Act (MCL §445.111) is one of the older state DNC statutes** and the AG operates a Michigan-specific DNC list in addition to the federal list. A mortgage lead-generation site that scrapes DNC status from the federal list alone is not in compliance with the Michigan DNC.

## 5.3 New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act (CFA) — N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.

**Citation / primary source:** **N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.**, the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. The CFA is enforced by the New Jersey Attorney General (and indirectly by the Division of Consumer Affairs) and is one of the **broadest and most plaintiff-friendly** state consumer-protection statutes in the United States.
- New Jersey Office of the Attorney General: https://www.njoag.gov/
- New Jersey Division of Consumer Affairs: https://www.njconsumeraffairs.gov/ (revalidate URL)

**Application to mortgage lead-gen:** the CFA reaches "any unconscionable commercial practice, deception, fraud, false pretense, false promise, misrepresentation" in the "sale or advertisement of any merchandise" or "the subsequent performance" thereof. The CFA has been the AG's primary vehicle for pursuing mortgage lead generators, mortgage brokers, reverse-mortgage companies, and debt-relief services. The CFA has been used in tandem with the New Jersey Home Ownership Security Act (§5.5 below) to pursue predatory lending.

**Treble damages and attorneys' fees:** the CFA provides for **treble damages** and **attorneys' fees** to a private plaintiff who can show an ascertainable loss. This is significantly more plaintiff-friendly than most state UDAP statutes.

**Recent enforcement (2023–2024):** the New Jersey AG has been very active in 2023–2024 against mortgage lead generators and "trigger lead" vendors. Notable 2023–2024 actions (revalidate against the AG press release archive):
- Multiple settlements with mortgage lead generators and trigger-lead vendors for selling leads to multiple lenders, misrepresenting the purpose of the consumer's information, and failing to honor DNC requests.
- Reverse-mortgage enforcement (in tandem with HUD and the CFPB).
- "Debt relief" and student-loan "forgiveness" enforcement that overlaps with mortgage lead-gen.

## 5.5 New Jersey Home Ownership Security Act — N.J.S.A. 46:10B-1 et seq. (predatory lending)

**Citation / primary source:** **N.J.S.A. 46:10B-1 et seq.**, the New Jersey Home Ownership Security Act (HOSA), is a predatory-lending statute enacted in the early 2000s. HOSA includes:
- **Steering** prohibitions (brokers/lenders may not steer consumers to loans with terms less favorable than those for which they qualify).
- **Rate and points** caps for high-cost loans.
- **Required disclosures** for high-cost loans.
- **Liability** for violations.

**Application to a mortgage qualification diagnostic:** HOSA's steering prohibition is a fit for any diagnostic that systematically produces results that benefit a particular lender, a particular loan product, or a particular loan term. The diagnostic should not "steer" consumers to a specific lender or product based on factors that the consumer has not asked about.

## 5.7 What is unique about New Jersey

- **DOBI is a combined regulator** (banking, insurance, and real estate lending) — one of a small number of state regulators with this structure. This means a single agency supervises banks, mortgage lenders, mortgage brokers, MLOs, insurance companies, and insurance producers. The DOBI's coordinated enforcement approach is broader than the typical "Department of Banking" or "Department of Insurance" state.
- **Treble damages and attorneys' fees** under the CFA (N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.) make New Jersey one of the most plaintiff-friendly states in the country for consumer-fraud actions. Class actions under the CFA are common in mortgage and lead-generation cases.
- **The 2024 New Jersey mini-TCPA amendments** significantly increased statutory damages (up to $1,000 per violation, to be revalidated) and expressly cover SMS/text marketing messages. (Specific 2024 bill number and effective date to be revalidated; the 2024 New Jersey mini-TCPA amendments are widely reported but the exact bill number and effective date should be confirmed against the live NJ Legislature database.)
- **HOSA (N.J.S.A. 46:10B-1 et seq.)** is one of the older state predatory-lending statutes. The steering prohibition is broadly worded and is a fit for any diagnostic that produces results that benefit a particular lender.
- **Trigger-lead enforcement:** the New Jersey AG has been at the national forefront of "trigger lead" enforcement — i.e., enforcement against companies that obtain a consumer's name from a credit-bureau "trigger" (e.g., the consumer's application for a credit card) and then sell that consumer's name as a mortgage lead to multiple lenders. A mortgage qualification diagnostic that obtains consumer data from a credit-bureau trigger and then routes the lead to one or more lenders is potentially subject to the AG's trigger-lead enforcement.
- **New Jersey has a state AG mini-TCPA** but no comprehensive state consumer-privacy law comparable to the Colorado Privacy Act, California CCPA/CPRA, Connecticut CTDPA, or Virginia VCDPA as of 2024–2025. (To be revalidated; a New Jersey privacy bill was under consideration in the 2024 session.)

You may inform the appraiser of your opinion of value, the borrower's opinion of value, or the list or sales price of the property. **You are prohibited from telling the appraiser the value you need or that is required for your loan to be successful.**
> (3) What business practices are prohibited? The following business practices are prohibited:
> (a) Directly or indirectly employing any scheme, device, or artifice to defraud or mislead borrowers or lenders or to defraud any person.
> (b) Engaging in any unfair or deceptive practice toward any person.
> (c) Obtaining property by fraud or misrepresentation.
> (d) Soliciting or entering into a contract with a borrower that provides in substance that the mortgage broker may earn a fee or commission through the mortgage broker's 'best efforts' to obtain a loan even though no loan is actually obtained for the borrower.
> (e) Charging discount points on a loan which does not result in a reduction of the interest rate. Some examples of discount point misrepresentations are: (i) A mortgage broker or lender charging discount points on the good faith estimate, loan estimate, or settlement statement payable to the mortgage broker or any party that is not the actual lender on the resident mortgage loan. (ii) Charging loan fees or mortgage broker fees that are represented to the borrower as discount points when such fees do not actually reduce the rate on the loan, or reflecting loan origination fees or mortgage broker fees as discount points. (iii) Charging discount points that are not mathematically determinable as the same direct reduction of the rate available to any two borrowers with the same program and underwriting characteristics on the same date of disclosure.
> (f) Failing to clearly and conspicuously disclose whether a payment advertised or offered for a residential mortgage loan includes amounts for taxes, insurance, or other products sold to the borrower. This prohibition includes the practice of misrepresenting, either orally, in writing, or in any advertising materials, a loan payment that includes only principal and interest as a loan payment that includes principal, interest, tax, and insurance.
> (g) Making or funding a loan by any means other than table funding.
> (h) Negligently making any false statement or willfully making any omission of material fact in connection with any application or any information filed by a licensee in connection with any application, examination or investigation conducted by the department. This includes leaving blanks on a document and instructing the borrower to sign the document with the blanks or providing the borrower with documents with blanks. You are not prohibited from marking some information blanks with 'N/A' if the information is not applicable to the transaction.
> (i) Willfully filing a lien on property without a legal basis to do so.
> (j) Coercing, intimidating, or threatening borrowers in any way with the intent of forcing them to complete a loan transaction.
> (k) Failing to make disclosures to loan applicants and noninstitutional investors as required by RCW 19.146.030 and any other applicable state or federal law.
> (l) Making, in any manner, any false or deceptive statement or representation with regard to the rates, points, or other financing terms or conditions for a residential mortgage loan. An example is advertising a discounted rate without clearly and conspicuously disclosing in the advertisement the cost of the discount to the borrower and that the rate is discounted.
> (m) **Engage in bait and switch advertising.** Bait and switch means a deceptive practice of soliciting or promising a loan at favorable terms, but later 'switching' or providing a loan at less favorable terms. While bait and switch will be determined by the facts of a case, the following examples, alone or in combination, may exhibit a bait and switch practice: (i) A deceptive change of loan program from fixed to variable rate. (ii) A deceptive increase in interest rate. (iii) The misrepresentation of discount points. …"

## 6.3 Washington Consumer Protection Act — RCW 19.86

**Citation / primary source:** **RCW 19.86** (the Washington Consumer Protection Act, "CPA"). The CPA is enforced by the Washington Attorney General and reaches "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in trade or commerce" and "unfair methods of competition."

**CPA-hook from the mortgage lending statute:** **RCW 31.04.027** (and the parallel RCW 19.146.020 and other mortgage-broker statutes) makes a violation of Washington's mortgage lending statute *automatically* a violation of the Washington CPA. This means a Washington-licensed mortgage broker or lender that violates any provision of the mortgage broker rules (including WAC 208-660 advertising, prohibited practices, or licensing rules) is *ipso facto* subject to CPA enforcement, including AG investigation, civil penalties (up to $7,500 per violation under RCW 19.86.090), and consumer restitution.

**Application to mortgage lead-gen:** the AG has used the CPA-hook to pursue mortgage brokers, lenders, and lead generators who violate the WAC 208-660 rules. The AG's press releases include 2023–2024 actions against lead generators and mortgage companies. Revalidate against https://www.atg.wa.gov/ for current press releases.

**Private right of action:** **RCW 19.86.090** — a private plaintiff may bring an action for actual damages; the AG may bring an action for the same plus injunctive relief and civil penalties of up to $7,500 per violation.

## 6.4 Washington Commercial Electronic Mail Act (CEMA) — RCW 19.190

**Citation / primary source:** **RCW 19.190** — Washington's Commercial Electronic Mail Act (CEMA). CEMA is one of the **oldest state anti-spam statutes** in the United States (enacted 1998) and was amended in 2003 to cover commercial electronic text messages (RCW 19.190.060).

Key sections (revalidate against the live RCW portal):
- **RCW 19.190.010 — Definitions** ("commercial electronic mail message," "electronic mail address," "initiate," "Internet domain name," "message recipient," "sender," "sexual predator identification," etc.).
- **RCW 19.190.020 — Commercial e-mail prohibitions** (e.g., use of a third party's internet domain name without permission; false or misleading information in the subject line; header information that is false or misleading).
- **RCW 19.190.030 — CPA hook** *(verbatim from RCW 19.190.030):* "(1) It is a violation of the consumer protection act, chapter 19.86 RCW, to conspire with another person to initiate the transmission or to initiate the transmission of a commercial electronic mail message that: (a) Uses a third party's internet domain name without permission of the third party, or otherwise misrepresents or obscures any information in identifying the point of origin or the transmission path of a commercial electronic mail message; or (b) Contains false or misleading information in the subject line. (2) It is a violation of the consumer protection act, chapter 19.86 RCW, to assist in the transmission of a commercial electronic mail message, when the person providing the assistance knows, or consciously avoids knowing, that the initiator of the commercial electronic mail message is engaged, or intends to engage, in any act or practice that violates the consumer protection act. (3) The legislature finds that the practices covered by this chapter are matters vitally affecting the public interest for the purpose of applying the consumer protection act, chapter 19.86 RCW. A violation of this chapter is not reasonable in relation to the development and business and is an unfair or deceptive act in trade or commerce and an unfair method of competition for the purpose of applying the consumer protection act, chapter 19.86 RCW."
- **RCW 19.190.040 — Sexual Predator Identification Act** (Washington's 1998 "Sexual Predator Identification Act" provision requires commercial e-mail to include "ADV:" in the subject line for advertisement; the "ADV" label was the original CEMA requirement; the "sexual predator" terminology was a then-controversial name for the act). The provision is now commonly referred to as the "ADV" labeling requirement.
- **RCW 19.190.060 — Commercial electronic text message — Prohibition on initiation or assistance** *(verbatim from RCW 19.190.060):* "(1) No person conducting business in the state may initiate or assist in the transmission of an electronic commercial text message to a telephone number assigned to a Washington resident for cellular telephone or pager service that is equipped with short message capability or any similar capability allowing the transmission of text messages. (2) The legislature finds that the practices covered by this section are matters vitally affecting the public interest for the purpose of applying the consumer protection act, chapter 19.86 RCW. A violation of this section is not reasonable in relation to the development and preservation of business and is an unfair or deceptive act in trade or commerce and an unfair method of competition for the purpose of applying the consumer protection act, chapter 19.86 RCW." (History: 2003 c 137 s 3. The legislative intent statement cites the cost and inconvenience to consumers of unsolicited commercial text messages.)

**Application to a mortgage qualification diagnostic:** any commercial e-mail sent to a Washington consumer must comply with CEMA (no use of third-party domain names without permission, no false or misleading header information, no false or misleading subject lines, and the "ADV:" subject-line labeling requirement). Any commercial text message sent to a Washington wireless number is per se prohibited unless the consumer has provided prior express consent (the federal TCPA provides the federal floor; CEMA is a parallel state-law prohibition that is automatically a CPA violation).

## 6.5 Washington My Health My Data Act (MHMDA) — RCW 19.373

**Citation / primary source:** **RCW 19.373** — the Washington My Health My Data Act, effective in stages in 2023 (effective date: March 31, 2024, for regulated entities other than small businesses; June 30, 2024, for small businesses; some provisions effective earlier). The MHMDA is a **state-level health-privacy statute** that is broadly applicable, even to non-health businesses, because it defines "consumer health data" to include any data that is "linked or reasonably linkable to a consumer and that identifies the consumer's past, present, or future physical or mental health status."

Key definitions (from RCW 19.373.010 — revalidate against the live RCW portal):
- **"Consumer health data"** is defined to include:
  1. Individual health conditions, treatment, diseases, or diagnosis;
  2. Social, psychological, behavioral, and medical interventions;
  3. Health-related surgeries or procedures;
  4. Use or purchase of prescribed medication;
  5. Bodily functions, vital signs, symptoms, or measurements;
  6. Diagnoses or diagnostic testing, treatment, or medication;
  7. Gender-affirming care information;
  8. Reproductive or sexual health information;
  9. Biometric data;
  10. Genetic data;
  11. **"Precise location information that could reasonably indicate a consumer's attempt to acquire or receive health services or supplies";**
  12. Data that identifies a consumer seeking health care services;
  13. **Any information that a regulated entity or a small business, or their respective processor, processes to associate or identify a consumer with the data described in (1) through (12) of this subsection that is derived or extrapolated from nonhealth information (such as proxy, derivative, inferred, or emergent data by any means, including algorithms or machine learning).**

- **"Consent"** is a clear affirmative act that signifies a consumer's freely given, specific, informed, opt-in, voluntary, and unambiguous agreement, **which may include written consent provided by electronic means**. Consent may not be obtained by (i) a general or broad terms of use agreement that contains descriptions of personal data processing along with other unrelated information, (ii) a consumer hovering over, muting, pausing, or closing a given piece of content, or (iii) a consumer's agreement obtained through the use of deceptive designs.
- **"Deceptive design"** is a user interface designed or manipulated with the effect of subverting or impairing user autonomy, decision making, or choice.

**Application to a mortgage qualification diagnostic — the "even though mortgage isn't health" question:**

The MHMDA was enacted to address gaps in health-privacy protection (HIPAA only covers covered entities and business associates). The MHMDA's "consumer health data" definition is broader than HIPAA's "protected health information" because it includes **"precise location information that could reasonably indicate a consumer's attempt to acquire or receive health services or supplies"** and **any information that … processes to associate or identify a consumer with the data described … that is derived or extrapolated from nonhealth information (such as proxy, derivative, inferred, or emergent data by any means, including algorithms or machine learning).**

A mortgage qualification diagnostic can trigger the MHMDA in three ways:

1. **Reproductive-health inference.** If the diagnostic uses any data (income, ZIP code, age, family composition, etc.) to *infer* a consumer's reproductive-health status — for example, by including a question about the number of dependents under 18, by including a question about marital status, or by accepting free-text data that might contain pregnancy- or fertility-related information — the resulting inference may be "consumer health data" under RCW 19.373.010(8)(b)(xiii).
2. **Geofencing around health-care facilities.** If the diagnostic (or any advertising for the diagnostic) geofences a health-care facility (e.g., a Planned Parenthood clinic, a fertility clinic, a mental-health provider, an abortion provider), the geofencing data may be "precise location information that could reasonably indicate a consumer's attempt to acquire or receive health services or supplies" under RCW 19.373.010(8)(b)(xi). **The MHMDA expressly prohibits geofencing health-care facilities.**
3. **Biometric data.** If the diagnostic uses any biometric authentication (facial recognition, voice, fingerprint, etc.) — even for account security — the biometric data is "consumer health data" under RCW 19.373.010(8)(b)(ix).

**Practical compliance steps:**
- Do not include questions about reproductive status, fertility, family-planning, pregnancy, or abortion in the diagnostic.
- Do not collect, infer, or store data about a consumer's health status.
- Do not use biometric authentication.
- Do not geofence any health-care facility for advertising or marketing.
- If a consumer voluntarily provides health-related information in a free-text field, redact it before processing or routing.
- If the diagnostic processes any data that could be inferred (via ML or otherwise) to be related to health, conduct a Data Protection Assessment under RCW 19.373.030 and obtain opt-in consent before collection.

## 6.9 What is unique about Washington

- **WAC 208-660 is one of the most detailed mortgage broker rule chapters in the United States.** It is exhaustive on advertising (WAC 208-660-440, -445, -446), prohibited practices (WAC 208-660-500), disclosures (WAC 208-660-430), recordkeeping (WAC 208-660-450), GLBA implementation (WAC 208-660-460, -470), and data-breach notification (WAC 208-660-480). A mortgage lead-generation site that operates in Washington should expect to be held to the full WAC.
- **CPA-hook from RCW 31.04.027:** a violation of any provision of Washington's mortgage-lending statutes (including WAC 208-660) is automatically an unfair or deceptive act in trade or commerce under the Washington Consumer Protection Act (RCW 19.86). The AG's enforcement authority is correspondingly broad.
- **CEMA (RCW 19.190) is one of the oldest state anti-spam statutes** (enacted 1998) and was amended in 2003 to cover commercial electronic text messages (RCW 19.190.060). CEMA is automatically a CPA violation. A mortgage qualification diagnostic that sends marketing e-mail or text to a Washington consumer must comply with CEMA's "ADV:" subject-line requirement, third-party domain-name prohibition, and per se prohibition on commercial text messages absent prior express consent.
- **MHMDA (RCW 19.373) is a broadly applicable health-privacy statute** that reaches non-health businesses (including mortgage businesses) through the "consumer health data" definition's inclusion of precise-location information that could indicate a consumer's attempt to acquire health services, and any data that is derived or extrapolated from non-health information to identify a consumer with health-related data. The MHMDA expressly prohibits geofencing health-care facilities.
- **Washington's My Health My Data Act is the first state-level "geofencing health-care" prohibition.** A mortgage diagnostic that uses any geofencing (or any data source that is geofencing-derived) for advertising or marketing is at material risk under RCW 19.373.
- **Washington's AI bill (SB 5838 (2024)) is currently a task force, not a comprehensive AI Act**; the existing compliance regime for AI in mortgage qualification is WAC 208-660-500, RCW 19.86, RCW 19.144, and federal law. The task force's recommendations (2025–2027) may, but have not yet, led to a comprehensive Washington AI Act.
- **Washington's consumer-protection regime is comprehensive and AG-aggressive.** The Washington AG has been one of the most active state AGs on AI, consumer-privacy, and consumer-finance issues in 2023–2024.

>
