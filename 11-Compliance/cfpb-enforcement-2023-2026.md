# 1. CFPB v. 1st Alliance Lending, LLC — RESPA Section 8 / MAP Rule / ECOA / FCRA / TILA (2021 filed, 2025 dismissed with prejudice)

## 1.1 Case Identification

- **Case name / docket:** *CFPB v. 1st Alliance Lending, LLC; John Christopher DiIorio; Kevin Robert St. Lawrence; Socrates Aramburu*; **United States District Court for the District of Connecticut, No. 3:21-cv-00080 (filed Jan. 15, 2021; amended complaint April 1, 2021).**
- **Regulator:** CFPB (sole plaintiff).
- **Initial filing date:** **January 15, 2021.**
- **Amended complaint:** **April 1, 2021.**
- **Status:** **Dismissed with prejudice on February 28, 2025** (joint stipulated dismissal; partial dismissal of claims against Aramburu was entered March 13, 2023 and docketed March 14, 2023).
- **CFPB enforcement-action page:** `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/enforcement/actions/1st-alliance-lending-llc-et-al/` (page last modified Feb. 20, 2026 @ 11:13 AM EST).
- **CFPB press release (archived):** "CFPB Sues 1st Alliance Lending, LLC and Its Principals for Alleged Unlawful Mortgage Lending Practices," Jan. 15, 2021.

## 1.2 Defendants

- **1st Alliance Lending, LLC** — a Connecticut-based residential mortgage lender that originated residential mortgages from 2004 to September 2019 and stopped operating in November 2019. (Per the CFPB enforcement-action page.)
- **John Christopher DiIorio** — CEO of 1st Alliance.
- **Kevin Robert St. Lawrence** — managing executive.
- **Socrates Aramburu** — managing executive. All claims against Aramburu were dismissed March 13, 2023 (joint stipulation).

## 1.3 Statutes and Rules Allegedly Violated

The CFPB's complaint (as amended April 1, 2021) alleged violations of:

- **Truth in Lending Act (TILA)**, 15 U.S.C. § 1601 *et seq.*, and **Regulation Z**, 12 C.F.R. Part 1026;
- **Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)**, 15 U.S.C. § 1681 *et seq.*;
- **Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA)**, 15 U.S.C. § 1691 *et seq.*, and **Regulation B**, 12 C.F.R. Part 1002;
- **MAP Rule (Regulation N)**, 12 C.F.R. Part 1014 (12 C.F.R. § 1014.3 — material misrepresentations in mortgage credit product advertisements, including misrepresentations about the consumer's ability to qualify, the terms and conditions of the credit product, and government affiliation);
- **Consumer Financial Protection Act of 2010 (CFPA)**, 12 U.S.C. § 5536, **unfair and deceptive acts or practices (UDAP)**.

## 1.4 Conduct Alleged

The complaint alleged that 1st Alliance, DiIorio, St. Lawrence, and Aramburu engaged in unfair and deceptive practices, including MAP Rule violations in mortgage advertising, TILA / Reg Z violations, FCRA violations, and ECOA / Reg B violations, in connection with the company's residential mortgage origination activity from 2004 through November 2019. The specific factual content of the complaint is in the **Complaint** and **Amended Complaint** documents on the CFPB enforcement-action page.

## 1.5 Resolution

- **March 13, 2023** — joint stipulation filed; certain counts and all claims against Socrates Aramburu dismissed (docketed March 14, 2023).
- **February 28, 2025** — pursuant to a stipulated dismissal, the court voluntarily dismissed the case against all remaining defendants **with prejudice** (docketed the same day).
- **No monetary payment** and no injunction were entered against the company or its executives. The dismissal is procedural and reflects the post-January 2025 CFPB leadership's enforcement-prioritization choice. **The underlying factual record and CFPB theory of liability (TILA, FCRA, ECOA, MAP Rule, CFPA UDAAP) remain the doctrinal anchor for live CFPB mortgage-origination enforcement.**

## 1.6 Significance for Lead Generators and Diagnostic / Qualification Sites

- The 1st Alliance complaint is **not a Section 8 / lead-purchase case**; it is a **TILA / FCRA / ECOA / MAP Rule / UDAAP** case against the lender itself. The case is in this report because (i) the user asked for it specifically, and (ii) the **MAP Rule** allegations — material misrepresentations in mortgage credit product advertising — are directly relevant to a diagnostic / qualification site's advertising, including "approval likelihood," "pre-approved," "pre-qualified," and "guaranteed" claims.
- For a diagnostic site, the operative 2024–2025 RESPA Section 8 / lead-purchase precedent is **CFPB v. Freedom Mortgage + Realty Connect USA** (Aug. 17, 2023; $1.75M + $200K; consent order still in effect as of 2025). The 1st Alliance case is not a Section 8 case.

# 2. 2024 REDLINING SETTLEMENTS — Fairway, CrossCountry, Cadence, and the Trump-Era Vacaturs

The 2024 redlining settlements are the leading-edge actions of the CFPB / DOJ **"Combating Redlining Initiative"** (announced by AG Garland in 2021). Per AG Garland's Oct. 15, 2024 statement on the Fairway settlement, the Initiative has secured **"over $150 million in relief"** in three years through "Combating Redlining Initiative" actions. Asst. AG Kristen Clarke added: "With more than $150 million in total relief secured in three short years, our Combating Redlining Initiative is generating real economic opportunity for communities of color while sending a strong message to mortgage lenders, no matter their business model, that discriminatory lending will not be tolerated in America."

## 2.1 Fairway Independent Mortgage Corporation — Oct. 15, 2024

- **Case name / docket:** *United States of America and CFPB v. Fairway Independent Mortgage Corporation*; **U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Alabama, No. 2:24-cv-01405 (filed Oct. 15, 2024; consent order entered Dec. 3, 2024).**
- **Regulators:** **CFPB + DOJ (Civil Rights Division, Housing and Civil Enforcement Section; U.S. Attorney's Office for the Northern District of Alabama).**
- **CFPB enforcement-action page:** `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/enforcement/actions/fairway-independent-mortgage-corporation/`
- **CFPB press release (archived at CFPB Public Archive):** "CFPB and Justice Department Take Action Against Fairway for Redlining Black Neighborhoods in Birmingham, Alabama," Oct. 15, 2024, `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-and-justice-department-take-action-against-fairway-for-redlining-black-neighborhoods-in-birmingham-alabama/`
- **Statutes / rules:** ECOA, 15 U.S.C. § 1691 *et seq.*, and Regulation B, 12 C.F.R. Part 1002; CFPA, 12 U.S.C. § 5536; **Fair Housing Act (FHA), 42 U.S.C. § 3601 *et seq.*** (DOJ claim).
- **Conduct alleged:** Fairway redlined majority-Black neighborhoods in the Birmingham-Hoover Metropolitan Statistical Area (Birmingham MSA). From 2015 through 2022, Fairway operated three retail loan offices and three loan production desks in real-estate offices, **all located in majority-white areas**. Fairway relied on referrals from real-estate professionals and others, the vast majority of which were located in majority-white areas. Fairway predominantly directed its marketing (including direct mail) to majority-white areas. **Only 3.7% of Fairway's applications from 2018 through 2022 were for properties in majority-Black areas, vs. 12.2% for Fairway's peer lenders. The disparity was even higher in neighborhoods with 80% or more Black residents, where Fairway made loans at less than an eighth of the rate of its peer lenders.** The complaint alleged that Fairway's own data showed it was failing to serve majority-Black neighborhoods, but that prior to October 2022 Fairway took no steps to address redlining risk other than telling loan officers not to discriminate, and had no written marketing or growth plan to address the concern.
- **Settlement / penalty:**
  - **$1.9 million civil penalty** paid to the CFPB's Civil Penalty Fund.
  - **$7 million for a loan-subsidy program** offering home-purchase, refinance, and home-improvement loans on a more affordable basis in majority-Black neighborhoods in the Birmingham MSA.
  - **At least $1 million to open or acquire a new loan-production office or full-service retail office in a majority-Black neighborhood** in the Birmingham MSA.
  - **At least $500,000 for advertising and outreach** to majority-Black neighborhoods.
  - **At least $250,000 on consumer financial education.**
  - **At least $250,000 on partnerships** with community-based or governmental organizations.
- **Diagnostic / AI tools covered:** The Fairway complaint focuses on **marketing-reach, branch-location, referral-source-network, and absence-of-written-marketing-plan** redlining — not on a specific algorithmic decision-tool. The CFPB's theory of liability is that Fairway's marketing and sales actions had the effect of denying equal access to credit in majority-Black neighborhoods. **The complaint does not name a specific AI / algorithmic underwriting tool.** For a diagnostic / qualification site, the Fairway fact pattern is the template for the "marketing-reach" redlining theory: even an online-only diagnostic site that does not deny any applicant is at risk if the marketing channel is not equally available to majority-minority ZIP codes.

## 2.8 Digital Redlining — the cross-cutting AI / digital theory

- The "digital redlining" theory articulated in the **April 25, 2023 Joint Statement on AI** (CFPB + DOJ + EEOC + FTC) treats a creditor's digital marketing, advertising targeting, and online service-delivery channels as within the scope of ECOA and the Fair Housing Act.
- The 2024 settlements (CrossCountry, Cadence, Truist) build on this theory.
- **Implication for diagnostic / qualification sites:** A site that (i) only operates online, (ii) only advertises in non-minority ZIP codes, (iii) only markets through channels not used by minority consumers, or (iv) uses algorithmic targeting to deliver mortgage advertising (e.g., Facebook "lookalike audiences," "Ethnic Affinity" / "Multicultural Affinity" categories) is at risk of a digital-redlining challenge even if the site itself does not deny any applicant. The site should advertise broadly, ensure equal access across all geographies, document its marketing reach, and maintain a fair-lending monitoring program that includes the digital channel.

# 3. DECEMBER 2024 CFPB INTERPRETIVE RULE ON DIGITAL COMPARISON-SHOPPING TOOLS

## 3.1 Bottom Line

**There is no finalized December 2024 CFPB interpretive rule on lead purchases or digital comparison-shopping tools in mortgage.** The CFPB's final-rule inventory at `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/final-rules/` does not include a December 2024 interpretive rule on lead purchases or comparison-shopping tools. The CFPB's rulemaking agenda (Reginfo.gov Statement 3170, Spring 2024, Fall 2024, Spring 2025, Fall 2025) does not include a December 2024 RESPA Section 8 / lead-purchase rulemaking. **The likely source of any "December 2024" reference is one of the following operative CFPB statements, which together comprise the current legal framework for digital comparison-shopping tools in mortgage.**

## 3.2 The Operative CFPB Statements on Digital Comparison-Shopping Tools

### 3.2.1 CFPB Advisory Opinion, Feb. 7, 2023 (88 Fed. Reg. 9,162 (Feb. 13, 2023)) — **NOT WITHDRAWN**

- **Citation:** 88 Fed. Reg. 9,162 (Feb. 13, 2023). CFPB Advisory Opinion, "Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (Regulation X); Digital Mortgage Comparison-Shopping Platforms and Related Payments to Operators."
- **CFPB press release:** "CFPB Issues Guidance to Protect Mortgage Borrowers from Pay-to-Play Digital Comparison-Shopping Platforms," Feb. 7, 2023, `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-issues-guidance-to-protect-mortgage-borrowers-from-pay-to-play-digital-comparison-shopping-platforms/`
- **Substance:** The advisory opinion addresses whether payments from mortgage lenders or brokers to operators of digital mortgage comparison-shopping platforms for the routing of consumer inquiries to specific lenders constitute "thing of value" under RESPA Section 8, 12 U.S.C. § 2607. The CFPB's position: payments that vary based on the routing or placement of consumer inquiries are payments for referrals under RESPA Section 8, regardless of how they are characterized.
- **Status (post-May 2025):** **Not withdrawn** in 90 Fed. Reg. 20,084 (May 12, 2025). The advisory opinion remains in effect.

### 3.2.2 CFPB Feb. 29, 2024 Credit-Card Comparison-Shopping Guidance — **NOT WITHDRAWN**

- **CFPB press release:** "CFPB Issues Guidance to Rein in Rigged Comparison-Shopping Results for Credit Cards and Other Financial Products," Feb. 29, 2024, `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-issues-guidance-to-rein-in-rigged-comparison-shopping-results-for-credit-cards-and-other-financial-products/`
- **Substance:** The CFPB provided guidance on credit-card comparison-shopping tools, warning that the use of dark patterns and manipulated results in comparison tools may violate federal law (UDAP / UDAAP under FTC Act § 5 and CFPA § 1036). The guidance is the credit-card analog to the Feb. 7, 2023 mortgage-platform advisory opinion.

### 3.2.3 CFPB Aug. 10, 2022 "Algorithmic Marketing and Advertising" Interpretive Rule (87 Fed. Reg. 48,094) — **WITHDRAWN May 12, 2025**

- **Citation:** 87 Fed. Reg. 48,094 (Aug. 10, 2022). "CFPB Issues Interpretive Rule on Digital Marketers."
- **Substance:** Provided that, when digital marketers are involved in the identification or selection of prospective customers for consumer financial products or services, or the selection or placement of content to affect consumer behavior, they are typically service providers under the Consumer Financial Protection Act. When their actions — such as using an algorithm to determine who to market products and services to — violate federal consumer financial protection law, they can be held accountable.
- **Status:** **Withdrawn in 90 Fed. Reg. 20,084 (May 12, 2025).**
- **Substantive reach after withdrawal:** The interpretive rule was an interpretation, not a substantive grant of authority. The CFPA's "service provider" definition in 12 U.S.C. § 5481(26) and the supervision / enforcement authority over service providers in 12 U.S.C. § 5483(5) are independent of the interpretive rule and remain in effect.

### 3.2.4 The Oct. 7, 2020 RESPA Section 8 FAQs (page last modified March 15, 2024)

- **Primary-source URL:** `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/compliance-resources/mortgage-resources/real-estate-settlement-procedures-act/real-estate-settlement-procedures-act-faqs/`
- **Substance:** The CFPB's authoritative current guidance on lead purchases. **Not withdrawn** in 90 Fed. Reg. 20,084. The FAQs include the operative MSA FAQ 2 ("Referral vs. marketing service" distinction) and MSA FAQ 4 (Examples of prohibited MSAs).

### 3.2.5 CFPB Compliance Bulletin 2015-05 (Marketing Services Agreements / lead buying) — **NOT WITHDRAWN**

- **Citation:** CFPB Compliance Bulletin 2015-05: RESPA Compliance and Marketing Services Agreements, Oct. 8, 2015, `https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201510_cfpb_compliance-bulletin-2015-05-respa-compliance-and-marketing-services-agreements.pdf`
- **Substance:** The CFPB's foundational statement on MSAs and lead-buying arrangements. The Bulletin rejects the bright-line "paying for a lead ≠ paying for a referral" framing and identifies the fact patterns that will be treated as disguised kickbacks.

## 3.3 Practical Implications for a Mortgage Diagnostic / Lead-Generation Site

- **If the site is a "digital comparison-shopping platform" for mortgages** (connects consumers to multiple lenders based on a comparison of loan options), the site is within the scope of the Feb. 7, 2023 advisory opinion (88 Fed. Reg. 9,162). Payments from lenders to the platform that vary based on routing or placement of consumer inquiries are payments for referrals under RESPA Section 8.
- **If the site is a single-MLO lead-generation site** (captures leads and routes them to one licensed MLO), the site is within the scope of the Oct. 7, 2020 RESPA Section 8 FAQs and CFPB Compliance Bulletin 2015-05. Payments from the MLO to the site that vary based on closed-loan volume are payments for referrals.
- **If the site is a digital advertiser / digital marketing service** for mortgage lenders, the site is no longer within the explicit scope of the (now-withdrawn) Aug. 10, 2022 interpretive rule, but the underlying CFPA service-provider authority remains in effect. The site is still subject to the CFPA's UDAAP authority and the FTC Act § 5.
- **If the site uses an AI tool to evaluate the consumer for credit** (i.e., to produce an "approval likelihood" or "pre-qualification" output), the site is within the scope of CFPB Circular 2022-03 and Circular 2023-03 (both remain in effect). If the consumer is denied and the consumer is treated as an "applicant" under Regulation B, the site must provide specific and accurate adverse-action reasons.
- **If the site advertises using targeted audiences** (Facebook "lookalike audiences," "Ethnic Affinity" / "Multicultural Affinity" categories, etc.), the site is at risk of a digital-redlining challenge even if the site itself does not deny any applicant. See § 2.8 above.

## 4.2 Documents Withdrawn (selected list)

The May 12, 2025 withdrawal includes the following items relevant to AI and mortgage:

- **August 10, 2022 "Algorithmic Marketing and Advertising" Interpretive Rule** (87 Fed. Reg. 48,094) — the "digital marketers are typically service providers under the CFPA" rule. **WITHDRAWN.**
- **April 3, 2023 Policy Statement on Abusive Conduct** — the policy statement interpreting "abusive" under 12 U.S.C. § 5531 (which referenced AI explicitly). **WITHDRAWN.**

## 4.4 Circular 2022-03 — Full Text (Adverse Action Notification Requirements in Connection With Credit Decisions Based on Complex Algorithms)

- **Citation:** **87 Fed. Reg. 35,864 (June 14, 2022); CFPB document released May 26, 2022. Document Number 2022-12729.** Citing regulation: 12 C.F.R. Part 1002 (Regulation B).
- **Primary-source URLs:** Federal Register: `https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2022-12729`; PDF: `https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_2022-03_circular_2022-05.pdf`.
- **Question presented (verbatim):**
  > "When creditors make credit decisions based on complex algorithms that prevent creditors from accurately identifying the specific reasons for denying credit or taking other adverse actions, do these creditors need to comply with the Equal Credit Opportunity Act's requirement to provide a statement of specific reasons to applicants against whom adverse action is taken?"
- **Response (verbatim):**
  > "Yes. ECOA and Regulation B require creditors to provide statements of specific reasons to applicants against whom adverse action is taken. Some creditors may make credit decisions based on certain complex algorithms, sometimes referred to as uninterpretable or 'black-box' models, that make it difficult—if not impossible—to accurately identify the specific reasons for denying credit or taking other adverse actions. The adverse action notice requirements of ECOA and Regulation B, however, apply equally to all credit decisions, regardless of the technology used to make them. Thus, ECOA and Regulation B do not permit creditors to use complex algorithms when doing so means they cannot provide the specific and accurate reasons for adverse actions."
- **Key analysis points (verbatim):**
  > "Statements that the adverse action was based on the creditor's internal standards or policies or that the applicant, joint applicant, or similar party failed to achieve a qualifying score on the creditor's credit scoring system are insufficient."
  > "Creditors who use complex algorithms, including artificial intelligence or machine learning, in any aspect of their credit decisions must still provide a notice that discloses the specific principal reasons for taking an adverse action. Whether a creditor is using a sophisticated machine learning algorithm or more conventional methods to evaluate an application, the legal requirement is the same: Creditors must be able to provide applicants against whom adverse action is taken with an accurate statement of reasons."
  > "A creditor cannot justify noncompliance with ECOA and Regulation B's requirements based on the mere fact that the technology it employs to evaluate applications is too complicated or opaque to understand. A creditor's lack of understanding of its own methods is therefore not a cognizable defense against liability for violating ECOA and Regulation B's requirements."
  > "The adverse action notice requirements of ECOA and Regulation B … apply equally to all credit decisions, regardless of the technology used to make them."
  > "As data use and credit models continue to evolve, creditors have an obligation to ensure that these models comply with existing consumer protection laws."

## 4.5 Circular 2023-03 — Full Text (Adverse Action Notification Requirements and Proper Use of Sample Forms)

- **Citation:** **89 Fed. Reg. 27,361 (April 17, 2024); CFPB document released Sept. 19, 2023. Document Number 2024-08003. Docket ID: CFPB-2024-0001.** Citing regulation: 12 C.F.R. Part 1002 (Regulation B).
- **Primary-source URL:** Federal Register: `https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2024-08003`.
- **Question presented (verbatim):**
  > "When using artificial intelligence or complex credit models, may creditors rely on the checklist of reasons provided in CFPB sample forms for adverse action notices even when those sample reasons do not accurately or specifically identify the reasons for the adverse action?"
- **Response (verbatim):**
  > "No, creditors may not rely on the checklist of reasons provided in the sample forms (currently codified in Regulation B) to satisfy their obligations under ECOA if those reasons do not specifically and accurately indicate the principal reason(s) for the adverse action. Nor, as a general matter, may creditors rely on overly broad or vague reasons to the extent that they obscure the specific and accurate reasons relied upon."
- **Specific examples (verbatim):**
  > "For instance, if a complex algorithm results in a denial of a credit application due to an applicant's chosen profession, a statement that the applicant had 'insufficient projected income' or 'income insufficient for amount of credit requested' would likely fail to meet the creditor's legal obligations. Even if the creditor believed that the reason for the adverse action was broadly related to future income or earning potential, providing such a reason likely would not satisfy its duty to provide the specific reason(s) for adverse action."
  > "if a creditor decides to lower the limit on, or close altogether, a consumer's credit line based on behavioral data, such as the type of establishment at which a consumer shops or the type of goods purchased, it would likely be insufficient for the creditor to simply state 'purchasing history' or 'disfavored business patronage' as the principal reason for adverse action. Instead, the creditor would likely need to disclose more specific details about the consumer's purchasing history or patronage that led to the reduction or closure, such as the type of establishment, the location of the business, the type of goods purchased, or other relevant considerations, as appropriate."

## 4.8 The 2024 AVM Quality Control Final Rule (joint CFPB / FRB / FDIC / NCUA / OCC)

- **Substance:** Final rule on quality control standards for Automated Valuation Models (AVMs) used in mortgage origination. The rule requires institutions using AVMs to have quality-control systems designed to ensure (i) a high level of confidence in the AVM estimate; (ii) protection against manipulation of data; (iii) avoidance of conflicts of interest; (iv) random sample testing and reviews; and (v) account for other factors determined to be appropriate. The rule applies to mortgage-origination AVMs.
- **CFPB press release:** "CFPB Approves Rule to Ensure Accuracy and Accountability in the Use of AI and Algorithms in Home Appraisals," June 24, 2024, `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/blog/cfpb-approves-rule-to-ensure-accuracy-and-accountability-in-the-use-of-ai-and-algorithms-in-home-appraisals/`
- **Effect on diagnostic / AI tools:** The rule does not apply to AI tools that are not used in the mortgage-origination valuation process. A pre-qualification diagnostic that does not produce a property valuation is not within the AVM rule. A diagnostic that does produce or rely on an AVM estimate is within the rule, and the lender using the AVM must comply.
- **Status:** In effect (effective on the first day of the calendar quarter following 12 months after Federal Register publication).

## 4.11 Summary: 2025 CFPB AI / Mortgage Posture

- **Circular 2022-03 and Circular 2023-03 remain in effect** and continue to bind creditors. A creditor's use of an AI / ML / "black-box" model is not a defense against ECOA / Reg B adverse-action-notice requirements. The creditor must still provide specific and accurate reasons.
- **The Aug. 10, 2022 digital-marketer interpretive rule and the April 2023 abusive-conduct policy statement are withdrawn.** The underlying CFPA authority over digital marketers and abusive conduct is unchanged.
- **The post-May 2025 CFPB posture de-prioritizes fair-lending enforcement absent direct evidence of overt discrimination.** The Townstone vacatur, Trident termination, Draper & Kramer no-action letter, and Trustmark / Citi terminations reflect this. HUD, DOJ, state AGs, and private plaintiffs continue to bring fair-lending actions.
- **The AVM Quality Control rule is in effect.** A mortgage-origination AVM must have a quality-control system.
- **The June 2024 AI / chatbot symposium and the April 2023 Joint Statement on AI remain the doctrinal anchor** for the proposition that AI is not exempt from federal consumer-finance law. The withdrawal of specific documents does not withdraw the underlying substantive law.

- **3 NYCRR Part 79 (mortgage banker advertising):**
  - 3 NYCRR § 79.5(a): Every advertisement must include the mortgage banker's NMLS unique identifier in close proximity.
  - 3 NYCRR § 79.5(b): No advertisement may contain any false, misleading, or deceptive statement, including: misrepresenting the terms, conditions, or charges of a loan; misrepresenting the relationship between the mortgage banker and the consumer; representing that a loan is "guaranteed" or "approved" when it is not; **using the words "pre-approved" unless the mortgage banker has actually underwritten the loan and the consumer has received a written pre-approval letter**.
  - 3 NYCRR § 79.5(c): Triggering terms — if an advertisement states a rate, payment amount, or loan amount, it must include the full APR, the term, and either the loan amount or down-payment percentage, in close proximity.
  - 3 NYCRR § 79.5(d): "Not a commitment to lend" or substantially similar language is required whenever a rate, payment, or qualification is advertised without a full application and underwriting.
- **NY General Business Law (GBL) § 349 and § 350:** NY's general false-advertising and deceptive-practices statutes. Private right of action under § 349.

## 8.3 Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) — Effective Feb. 1, 2026

- **Citation:** Colo. Rev. Stat. § 6-1-1701 *et seq.*
- **Substance:** Imposes obligations on developers and deployers of "high-risk" AI systems, with "high-risk" AI systems including AI used for "essential" decisions, including housing, employment, education, and financial services. A mortgage qualification tool that uses AI to make decisions or recommendations is likely a "high-risk" AI system under the Colorado AI Act.
- **Requirements:** Reasonable risk management, consumer disclosures, anti-bias testing, and the obligation to provide consumers with the right to correct data used by the AI and to receive an explanation of the AI's role in the decision.
