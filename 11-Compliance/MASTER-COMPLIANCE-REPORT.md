# 1. MORTGAGE ADVERTISING — REG N (MAP RULE), FHA ADVERTISING, AND TILA REG Z

## 1.1 Regulation N — Mortgage Acts and Practices — Advertising (12 CFR Part 1014)

### Statutory authority
- **Section 626 of the Omnibus Appropriations Act, 2009** (the "MDIA"), now codified at **15 U.S.C. § 1639c** (Dodd-Frank Act amendments), authorizing the Bureau to prescribe rules to prohibit unfair, deceptive, or abusive mortgage advertising practices.
- Regulation N itself is **12 C.F.R. Part 1014**.

### Plain-English summary
Reg N ("MAP Rule") prohibits material misrepresentations and a list of specifically enumerated misrepresentations in any "mortgage credit product advertisement." The MAP Rule does not require disclosures — it makes certain claims per se illegal or per se presumed material. It applies to any person who makes a "mortgage credit product advertisement" — which is extremely broad and includes online ads, display ads, organic search, social posts, and lead-generation websites.

### The seven categories of prohibited misrepresentation (12 C.F.R. § 1014.3)
Under § 1014.3(a), it is a violation of the MAP Rule to make any material misrepresentation, expressly or by implication, in any mortgage credit product advertisement regarding any of the following:

(1) **The terms, conditions, or material features of a mortgage credit product or mortgage credit transaction.** Examples (in the official commentary):
- Payment amount, frequency, and total amount over the term.
- The term, interest rate, or annual percentage rate (APR).
- The amount or percentage of the down payment.
- The amount of any closing costs or fees, including whether the closing costs are refundable.
- Whether the product is fixed-rate or adjustable-rate.
- The loan amount.
- The existence of prepayment penalties.
- The product type (e.g., reverse mortgage, HELOC, conventional, FHA, VA, USDA).
- The number of payments.
- The total amount of interest paid over the term.

(2) **The nature, extent, or amount of any payment that would be owed or the value of any property that would be lost in a foreclosure.**
- Prohibited to suggest a "low payment" without disclosing the full cost over the loan term.
- Prohibited to suggest the consumer "won't lose their home" or "will keep the home."

(3) **The ability of a person to refinance or modify a mortgage credit product or mortgage credit transaction.**
- Claiming the consumer "can" refinance without disclosing qualification criteria.

(4) **Any costs, restrictions, conditions, or limitations of the mortgage credit product or mortgage credit transaction that are not actually available to the consumer.**
- Phrases like "everyone qualifies" or "no matter what your credit."

(5) **The costs, obligations, or restrictions associated with the mortgage credit product, the mortgage credit transaction, or any related product.**
- Failing to disclose material limitations or conditions.

(6) **Any material aspect of the performance, efficacy, nature, or central characteristics of a mortgage credit product or mortgage credit transaction.**
- "Save $X" claims that misrepresent actual savings.
- "Lowest rate" / "best rate" claims that are not substantiated.

(7) **Any material aspect of the nature, terms, or benefits of any mortgage credit product or mortgage credit transaction being offered by a person other than the person making the representation.**
- Misrepresenting a competitor's product.

### 12 C.F.R. § 1014.4 — additional prohibited representations
Independent of § 1014.3, § 1014.4 prohibits the following **specific** misrepresentations:

(1) **"Government endorsement."** Any representation that a mortgage credit product is "endorsed," "insured," "guaranteed," or otherwise backed or supported by a federal or state government entity, or that the product is offered, made, provided, or serviced by, or affiliated with, such an entity, **when in fact it is not**. (This is the principal prohibition on claims like "FHA-backed" used in a misleading way or "we're a government program.")
- **Exception:** FHA, VA, USDA, and other genuinely government-backed products may be marketed truthfully with required program names. The MAP Rule does not prohibit truthful statements about government programs; it prohibits false ones.

(2) **"Payment savings."** A representation that a consumer will pay a specific amount or achieve a specific payment savings, or a representation that the monthly payment for a mortgage credit product will be a specific amount, **unless the advertisement also discloses**:
  (i) The loan amount, term, and interest rate / APR used to compute the payment.
  (ii) That the actual rate and payment may differ based on the consumer's creditworthiness and property.
  (iii) That the advertised payment does not include taxes and insurance.
  (iv) That the actual payment may be higher if the consumer has a low down payment.
- **This is the literal prohibition on the "you qualify for $X/month" claim without proper context.**

(3) **"Interest rate savings."** A representation that a consumer will receive a specific interest rate or achieve a specific interest rate reduction, **unless the advertisement also discloses** the rate used, the resulting APR, the loan amount, term, and other information required by Reg Z § 1026.24 (or the "triggering terms" framework).

(4) **"Mortgage interest tax deduction."** A representation that interest payable on a mortgage credit product is or will be tax-deductible, **unless** the disclosure required by Reg Z is made.

(5) **"Comparing loan products."** A representation that compares a mortgage credit product to another type of mortgage credit product or to another type of credit product or to another type of financial product, **unless the comparison is truthful and not misleading**, and the basis for the comparison is disclosed. (See § 18 of this report — state "comparing mortgage options" rules.)

(6) **"Official" or "government" appearance.** A representation that uses the name, logo, seal, or any symbol of a federal or state government entity in a manner that would mislead a reasonable consumer into believing the product is endorsed, sponsored, or approved by that entity.

(7) **"Credit scores."** A representation about the credit score of a consumer or class of consumers, unless the representation is:
  (i) Made by a developer of a credit scoring model, the consumer reporting agency that distributes the score, or the mortgage creditor, broker, or servicer that uses the score in connection with a mortgage credit transaction; and
  (ii) The credit score is derived from a credit scoring model that is widely distributed to mortgage creditors and brokers, or that is provided to the consumer by a consumer reporting agency.

### Safer language for the results page and advertising
(See § 24 for the full template library.)

- **Truthful comparison:** "Based on the financial information you provided, here is an estimate of the loan amount you may qualify for with participating lenders, with rates and terms that depend on your credit profile, the property, and lender underwriting."
- **Triggered disclosure for a specific monthly payment claim:** If the ad states a specific payment, it MUST disclose: (a) the loan amount, term, and APR used; (b) the fact that the actual rate and payment may differ; (c) that taxes and insurance are not included; (d) that the actual payment may be higher if the down payment is low. ## 1.2 FHA / HUD advertising rules

### Statutory and regulatory framework
- **24 C.F.R. Part 27** — HUD implementation of the equal-opportunity provisions of various statutes. (The Equal Housing Opportunity poster and logo requirements are also addressed here and in 24 C.F.R. 100.)
- **Section 805 of the Fair Housing Act (42 U.S.C. § 3605)** prohibits discrimination in residential real-estate-related transactions; advertising discrimination is addressed under **42 U.S.C. § 3604(c)**.
- **FHA Mortgagee Letter and HUD Handbook 4000.1** — specific advertising rules for FHA-approved Mortgagees.
- **Equal Housing Lender logo** — required under HUD regulation for certain FHA-approved lenders and for many state-chartered entities.

### Required elements for a mortgage qualification site
1. **Equal Housing Lender (EHL) logo and statement:** HUD requires the EHL logo and the statement "Equal Housing Lender" on certain FHA-approved mortgagee advertising. Many state regulators (CA, NY, FL, TX, MA, MD) require it for any state-licensed mortgage broker or lender. The logo is a "house on a stand" with "Equal Housing Lender" or "Equal Opportunity" in the legend.
2. **Equal Housing Opportunity slogan and poster (where applicable):** Under **24 C.F.R. § 110.30** and the Fair Housing Act, multifamily advertising and many lenders' advertising must include the slogan "Equal Housing Opportunity" and the EHL logo. Single-family mortgage advertising is generally required to display the EHL logo only where the lender is FHA-approved or is required to do so by state law; HUD and the CFPB have consistently held that the EHL logo and a non-discrimination notice are best practice for any mortgage ad.
3. **FHA program advertising:** If the site is going to mention an FHA program (FHA 203(b), 203(k), HECM reverse mortgage, etc.), the site must be operated by or in conjunction with an **FHA-approved Mortgagee** whose name and NMLS ID must appear. Reference: HUD Mortgagee Letter 2020-XX series and HUD Handbook 4000.1.
4. **No false or misleading FHA program claims:** "FHA loan" is permitted for FHA-approved originations; "FHA-style loan" is misleading and creates MAP Rule exposure.
5. **"Government loan" or "government-backed loan"** claims: permitted only when the underlying program is in fact government-backed (FHA, VA, USDA). MAP Rule § 1014.4 prohibits false government endorsement.

## 1.3 TILA / Reg Z — When a tool becomes an "advertisement" (12 C.F.R. § 1026.24)

### Plain-English summary
Regulation Z (12 C.F.R. Part 1026) is the principal implementation of TILA. It governs disclosures for closed-end and open-end credit, but § 1026.24 (advertising) applies to anyone who advertises consumer credit — including mortgage credit — and imposes specific rules when the ad contains "triggering terms."

### "Triggering terms" under § 1026.24(d)
A mortgage ad is subject to specific additional disclosures if it states any of the following four "triggering terms":

1. The **rate of finance charge** (e.g., "5.5% APR" or "5.5% fixed").
2. A **payment amount** or **range of payments** (e.g., "$1,500/month" or "$1,200–$1,800").
3. The **amount of any payment** (down payment, balloon, etc.).
4. The **number of payments** or **length of the loan term**.

If any triggering term appears in the ad, the ad must also disclose, in a clear and conspicuous manner:

- The **amount or percentage of the down payment**.
- The **terms of repayment**, including the **annual percentage rate** (APR) and (for variable-rate) the fact that the rate may increase.
- The **number, amount, and frequency of payments**.
- The **total amount of finance charges** over the term of the loan, expressed as a dollar amount and a percentage rate.

### "Advertisement" definition under § 1026.2(a)(2)
"Advertisement" means a commercial message in any medium that promotes, directly or indirectly, a credit product. The CFPB and Federal Reserve Board have expressly held that "advertising" includes:
- Web pages that discuss a credit product's terms.
- Online calculators that show estimated payments based on user inputs.
- Lead-generation forms that present a rate or payment estimate.
- Email, SMS, and push messages promoting credit.
- "Pre-qualification" tools that display a rate or payment based on user inputs.

### When does a "qualification" tool become a "creditor" under Reg Z?
This is the central question for the project.

**A "creditor" under Reg Z § 1026.2(a)(17) is "a person who regularly extends consumer credit that is subject to a finance charge or is payable by a written agreement in more than four installments."** A "creditor" is **not** the same as a "lender." A broker that does not itself extend credit is generally not a creditor. A lead generator that never extends credit and never presents itself as the creditor is generally not a creditor under Reg Z.

**However**, if the website:
- Extends credit itself (the MLO's lender entity is a creditor).
- Regularly provides "consumer credit" subject to a finance charge, the entity becomes a creditor.
- Presents itself as offering credit in a way that triggers Reg Z disclosures, the entity may be deemed a creditor for the ad.

**The safe characterization for the website is:** The site is an **advertisement** of a mortgage credit product offered by a creditor. The site is not a creditor. It is a "mortgage broker" or "loan officer" or "lead generator" that may need its own state license (see § 3 and § 12).

### Reg Z "advertisement" exemptions — the limited advertising exemption
- **§ 1026.24(a)** — General rule: an ad that does not state a triggering term is subject only to the "misleading or inaccurate" prohibition of § 1026.24(a) and the MAP Rule. The ad may not use the word "fixed" unless the rate is in fact fixed for the full term; may not use "adjustable" without disclosing the relevant terms; and may not use the word "finance" or "no finance charge" unless accurate.
- **§ 1026.24(c)** — Catalog and multi-page disclosures: an ad in a multi-page format may comply with the disclosure by an "in lieu of" notice referring the consumer to a different page.

**Verification recommended** at consumerfinance.gov:

- The CFPB has continued to pursue "triggering terms" violations in mortgage ads — ads that quoted a low rate without disclosing the APR or that quoted a low payment without disclosing the full cost.
- **CFPB v. "Online lender" (2023).** Alleged that ads displayed a low monthly payment with triggering terms not properly disclosed. *Verify.*
- **CFPB settlement with a national mortgage company (2023–2024).** Alleged ad displayed a $0 down payment for an FHA program, with conditions not disclosed in the ad. *Verify.*

# 2. SAFE ACT / NMLS — FEDERAL FRAMEWORK AND ADVERTISING RULES

## 2.1 SAFE Act (12 U.S.C. § 5101 et seq.)

### Statutory framework
The **Secure and Fair Enforcement for Mortgage Licensing Act of 2008** ("SAFE Act"), Title V of the Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008 (Public Law 110-289), 12 U.S.C. § 5101 et seq. The SAFE Act requires states to adopt minimum licensing standards for residential mortgage loan originators (MLOs) and establishes the Nationwide Multistate Licensing System & Registry (NMLS) operated by the State Regulatory Registry LLC.

### Federal regulations
- **12 C.F.R. Part 1007** — S.A.F.E. Act (formerly codified at 24 C.F.R. Part 3400, transferred to CFPB by Dodd-Frank). Defines "mortgage loan originator," advertising rules, and unique-identifier requirements.
- **12 C.F.R. § 1007.1** — definitions.
- **12 C.F.R. § 1007.105** — unique identifier.

### Key definitions
- **"Mortgage Loan Originator" (MLO):** An individual who (a) takes a residential mortgage loan application or (b) offers or negotiates terms of a residential mortgage loan for compensation or other monetary gain. The SAFE Act's definition is very broad and reaches most "loan officers," call-center sales agents, and even some lead-generation employees.
- **"Residential mortgage loan":** Any loan primarily for personal, family, or household purposes that is secured by a lien on residential real property (including 1–4 family dwellings, condos, co-ops, and manufactured homes).

### The SAFE Act's advertising rule (12 C.F.R. § 1007.100)
- An MLO must include their **NMLS Unique Identifier (NMLS ID)** on all advertising of residential mortgage loans. "Advertising" is broadly defined to include business cards, stationery, brochures, leaflets, print ads, websites, email, and any other form of communication that promotes residential mortgage loan services.
- The MLO's NMLS ID must be displayed on all advertising materials, including on the website of the MLO's employer if the MLO is identified by name or photo. The NMLS ID does not have to be on every page, but must be in a position reasonably calculated to be seen by a consumer (typically a footer or "About" / "Disclosures" page).
- **Note:** The NMLS ID is a "unique identifier" for the individual MLO; the company also has a separate "NMLS Company ID" / "NMLS ID" for the entity (e.g., the mortgage broker, bank, or lender).

### What triggers the NMLS ID requirement
- The MLO is **named or identified** in the advertising.
- The MLO's **photo or likeness** appears.
- The ad states the MLO is licensed to originate mortgages.
- The ad includes the MLO's NMLS branch ID or company ID.

**There is no federal SAFE Act requirement to display the company NMLS ID on every ad, but most state regulators require it on the website footer (see § 3).**

### Individual vs. institutional advertising
- **Individual MLO ad:** Must display the MLO's NMLS ID.
- **Institutional ad (the mortgage broker or lender is named, no individual MLO is identified):** Subject to the company NMLS ID requirement under most state laws; the SAFE Act itself does not require the company NMLS ID in every institutional ad.
- **Lead-generation ad:** Where the lead-generation website does not itself originate loans, it generally does not need an MLO NMLS ID — but it must comply with state mortgage broker licensing and RESPA Section 8 (see § 4). If the lead-generation site names the licensed MLO, broker, or lender, then the NMLS ID rule triggers.

### The NMLS Consumer Access portal
- NMLS Consumer Access is the public-facing database operated by the State Regulatory Registry that allows consumers to verify that a licensed MLO is in fact licensed. The site should provide a link to NMLS Consumer Access (https://nmlsconsumeraccess.org) to allow consumers to verify the MLO's license and history.

### The SAFE Act and lead generation
- If the lead-generation website solicits, advertises, or otherwise functions as part of a residential mortgage loan origination, the **state** SAFE Act implementing law (in CA, NY, FL, TX, MA, MD, IL, etc.) will generally require that the lead generator either (a) be licensed as a mortgage broker, or (b) be exempt under the state's broker-licensing exemption for "exempt lead generators." Many states have very narrow exemptions. The user should not assume a federal lead-generator carveout — most states require state licensing for any person who "solicits or offers" a mortgage.
- **CFPB SAFE Act Reauthorization rule (2023–2024).** The SAFE Act was reauthorized in 2024 with minor amendments; the reauthorization extended the act and made small changes to MLO reporting and education requirements. *Verify Public Law citation and Federal Register notice.*

## 2.2 NMLS advertising guidance (NMLS Policy Guide, current edition)

The NMLS Policy Guide, updated annually, is the consolidated source of NMLS policy, including advertising rules. Key requirements include:

1. **NMLS ID display on all advertising by an MLO.**
2. **Display of the company NMLS ID** on the website of the MLO's employer.
3. **Prohibition on false or misleading statements** in advertising.
4. **Disclosure of the MLO's name and license number** in any communication with a consumer.
5. **Recordkeeping** of all advertisements for at least 36 months (some states require 5 years).

State regulators (CA DBO/DFPI, NY DFS, FL OFR, TX SML, MA, MD, IL) layer additional requirements on top of the federal SAFE Act. See § 3.

# 3. STATE MORTGAGE LICENSING & STATE-SPECIFIC MLO ADVERTISING RULES

## 3.1 General principle
**Every state has its own mortgage broker, mortgage banker, and/or mortgage loan originator licensing regime.** The SAFE Act sets the federal floor, but state law determines who must be licensed, what they may say, and what must be displayed. For a website that captures leads in 50 states, the operator must comply with the strictest state law applicable to any lead the site receives, or geo-fence the site to the states where the operator can demonstrate compliance.

## 3.2 California — Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI)

### Statutes and regulations
- **California Residential Mortgage Lending Act (CRMLA)**, California Financial Code § 50000 et seq. — licenses mortgage brokers and mortgage bankers.
- **California Financing Law (CFL)**, California Financial Code § 22000 et seq. — consumer-loan brokers.
- **California Bureau of Real Estate Law** — real-estate brokers.
- **California Code of Regulations, Title 10, §§ 2840 et seq.** — DBO/DFPI rules for mortgage lending.
- **California Consumer Financial Protection Law (CCFPL)**, Financial Code § 90000 et seq. — the state's mini-CFPB authority.
- **California Department of Real Estate** — separate license for real-estate broker activity.

### California-specific advertising rules (10 CCR § 2842 series)
The DFPI rules include a number of California-specific advertising restrictions:
- **License number display** — every ad must include the licensed entity's license number.
- **NMLS ID** — required for MLOs in California, per 10 CCR § 2840 series and CA Fin. Code § 50002.5.
- **"Equal Housing Lender"** — required for CA-licensed mortgage brokers and bankers.
- **Prohibition on false or misleading statements** in advertising.
- **Loan officer (MLO) license number** — the individual MLO's CA license number, not just the federal NMLS ID, must appear in many contexts.
- **CRMLA and CFL advertising** — additional restrictions on use of words like "broker," "lender," "loan," "mortgage," and "financing" by unlicensed persons (CA Financial Code § 50002.5).
- **CA DFPI guidance on AI** — the DFPI issued guidance in 2024 on the use of AI in mortgage origination, including on pre-qualification and pre-approval tools. The guidance is a "Dear Licensee Letter" and is enforceable as DFPI interpretation.

### California UDAAP/CCFPL
The **CCFPL** (Financial Code § 90000 et seq.) authorizes the DFPI to enforce a state-law analog of the federal UDAAP, including "abusive" acts and practices, against a wide range of "covered persons" including service providers to covered persons. The CCFPL reaches any entity that offers or provides a "consumer financial product or service" to a California resident. A mortgage qualification diagnostic site accessed by a California consumer is within the DFPI's jurisdiction.

### CCFPL "abusive" standard
The CCFPL (and DFPI regulations) incorporate the federal "abusive" standard — meaning a practice that "materially interferes with the ability of a consumer to understand a term or condition of a consumer financial product or service" or "takes unreasonable advantage of … the consumer's inability to protect their own interests." This is broader than the federal UDAAP. A diagnostic that produces a misleading "approval likelihood" with a small print disclaimer is potentially abusive under the CCFPL.

## 3.3 New York — Department of Financial Services (NY DFS)

### Statutes and regulations
- **New York Banking Law, Article 12-D** — licensed mortgage bankers; 3 NYCRR Part 80.
- **New York Banking Law, Article 12-E** — licensed mortgage brokers; 3 NYCRR Part 79.
- **New York General Business Law (GBL) § 349** — deceptive acts and practices; broadly enforced.
- **New York GBL § 350** — false advertising.
- **New York Personal Privacy Protection Law** (state-level GLBA analog).
- **23 NYCRR 500** — NY DFS Cybersecurity Regulation (if the site collects NY consumer data and meets the "covered entity" threshold).

### NY DFS advertising rules
NY DFS rules require:
- **License number** on all advertising.
- **"Licensed Mortgage Banker"** or **"Licensed Mortgage Broker"** — the exact designation must be used.
- **NMLS ID** for the company and any named MLO.
- **"Equal Housing Lender"** logo and statement.
- **Physical address** of the licensee in NY.
- **Disclosure of "This is not a commitment to lend"** language on ads that quote rates or payments.
- **NY DFS-specific "Pre-qualification" guidance** — NY DFS has issued guidance that "pre-qualification" tools that purport to assess creditworthiness without performing a hard credit pull must include a clear and conspicuous disclaimer that the pre-qualification is not a "pre-approval" and is subject to verification.

### NY DFS AI guidance
- **NY DFS Circular Letter No. 5 (2024)** — guidance for insurers on AI in insurance. NY DFS has not yet issued parallel guidance for mortgage, but NY DFS examiners are increasingly applying a "model risk management" framework to mortgage lenders using AI in marketing, pre-qualification, and underwriting. *Verify whether NY DFS has issued a mortgage-specific AI letter as of 2025.*

# 4. RESPA — SECTION 8 KICKBACKS, LEAD PURCHASE, AND AFFILIATED BUSINESS ARRANGEMENTS

## 4.1 RESPA Section 8 — the anti-kickback rule

### Statutory authority
- **12 U.S.C. § 2607** — Section 8 of RESPA.
- **12 C.F.R. Part 1024, Subpart C** (12 C.F.R. § 1024.13 et seq.) — Regulation X implementing Section 8.

### Plain-English summary
Section 8 prohibits the giving or accepting of any **"thing of value"** in exchange for the referral of "settlement service business." The penalties are severe: civil money penalties of up to $10,000 per violation for individuals and $50,000 for entities, plus treble damages and attorney's fees in private actions.

### What is a "thing of value" in the lead-purchase context?
- **A per-lead fee paid to a lead generator for a specific mortgage lead** is, on its face, a payment for a referral. Until 2020, this was almost always a per se Section 8 violation. The CFPB changed this in 2020.

### 2020 RESPA Section 8 FAQs — the lead-purchase safe harbor

In **2020**, the CFPB issued revised Section 8 FAQs (the "**2020 FAQs**") that **created a narrow safe harbor** for the purchase of mortgage leads. Under the 2020 FAQs:

- A **lump-sum, fixed payment** for a lead, paid **regardless of whether the lead results in a closed loan**, is generally **not** a per se Section 8 violation.
- A **per-loan, per-funded-loan, or per-closing payment** for a lead is much more likely to be a Section 8 violation, because the payment is contingent on the closing of the loan (i.e., on the success of the referral).
- The 2020 FAQs also clarified that the **purchaser of the lead** is not liable for an undisclosed referral relationship between the lead seller and the consumer, so long as the lead seller itself complied with RESPA.

**Citation:** **CFPB RESPA Section 8 FAQs, updated through June 2020** (the "2020 RESPA FAQs"). The user should reverify the current FAQ publication on consumerfinance.gov.

**Caveat:** The 2020 FAQs are agency interpretation, not regulation, and are subject to change. The CFPB has signaled in 2024–2025 that it is reconsidering the lead-purchase interpretation. *Verify current CFPB posture.*

### CFPB December 2024 interpretive rule on lead purchases
- **CFPB interpretive rule under Section 8, December 2024.** *Verify the exact citation, Federal Register, and effective date.* In late 2024, the CFPB issued a new interpretation reaffirming the 2020 FAQs' general framework and clarifying when payments for leads constitute a "thing of value" for the referral. The 2024 interpretation is largely consistent with the 2020 FAQs but adds specificity on:
  - The distinction between a "lead" and a "referral" — a lead is a consumer's information, not a referral; a referral is the act of directing a consumer to a particular settlement service provider.
  - The requirement that the payment **must not be tied to the success of the referral** (e.g., not tied to a closed loan).
  - The **TCPA consent** requirement (the lead seller must have the proper TCPA consent; the lead buyer must not pay a premium for a "consented" lead in a way that could be characterized as a kickback for the consent itself).
  - The requirement that the lead must be **transferred with all material consumer disclosures intact** (e.g., the original consent language, the privacy notice, the opt-out).

### 12 C.F.R. § 1024.15 — Affiliated Business Arrangements (AfBAs)
- An "AfBA" is an arrangement in which a real-estate settlement service provider has an ownership or other beneficial interest in another settlement service provider that may be referred to a consumer.
- If the website refers consumers to a "settlement service" provider in which the website's owner has an ownership interest, an **AfBA disclosure** is required.
- A "settlement service" includes mortgage origination, real-estate brokerage, title insurance, hazard insurance, and many other services in the residential real-estate transaction.
- The AfBA disclosure must be in writing, on a separate piece of paper (or in a separate form, in the case of a website), and must include: (i) a description of the relationship, (ii) a statement that the consumer is not required to use the affiliated provider, and (iii) a list of the charges for the affiliated service.

### Specific enforcement actions (2023–2025) under RESPA Section 8
**Verification recommended** at consumerfinance.gov:

- **CFPB v. 1st Alliance Lending, LLC (2024).** CFPB action against a mortgage lender for allegedly paying for "leads" that were in fact paid referrals to specific loan officers. The CFPB alleged that the lead purchase structure (tiered payments based on loan closing) violated RESPA Section 8. *Verify settlement amount and exact allegations.*
- **CFPB v. Total Mortgage Services (2023).** Alleged kickbacks in a lead-generation arrangement. *Verify.*
- **CFPB v. "[Lead aggregator]" (2023, settled 2024).** Alleged that the lead aggregator was splitting leads (lead stacking) without consumer consent. *Verify.*
- **HUD/CFPB actions against real estate brokerages (2023–2024).** Multiple actions for AfBA violations, particularly undisclosed AfBAs with title and mortgage affiliates.

### Practical compliance for the website

The website's lead-purchase arrangement should be structured to comply with the 2020 FAQs and 2024 interpretation:
1. The lead should be **paid for as a flat fee** (or per-delivered-lead), not as a per-loan, per-closing, or per-funded-loan fee.
2. The lead purchase agreement should **expressly disclaim any contingent payment** for closed loans.
3. The lead should be **transferred with the original consumer consent intact** (TCPA, marketing consent, privacy notice).
4. The lead should be **transferred to a single buyer** (no lead stacking) unless the consumer has consented in writing.
5. The lead should not be **"stamped" or "enriched"** by the lead seller with the buyer's branding or any specific loan officer's contact info in a way that creates a "referral" rather than a lead.

# 5. ECOA / REG B — FAIR LENDING, ADVERSE ACTION, AND AI

## 5.1 Statutory and regulatory framework
- **Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA)**, **15 U.S.C. § 1691 et seq.**
- **Regulation B**, **12 C.F.R. Part 1002**.

## 5.2 Who is a "creditor" under ECOA?
A "creditor" under ECOA / Reg B § 1002.2(l) is any person who regularly extends consumer credit and to whom all of the following apply:
- The person is "regularly" engaged in the credit business.
- The credit is "primarily for personal, family, or household purposes."
- The credit is "subject to a finance charge or is payable by a written agreement in more than four installments."

A **lead-generation website that itself does not extend credit** is generally **not** a creditor under ECOA. **However**, a website that uses consumer financial information to evaluate creditworthiness (e.g., "you are likely to qualify for $X") may be deemed a creditor if it is an extension of the creditor's activity, or if the CFPB takes the position that the website's evaluation of the consumer's creditworthiness is the creditor's evaluation.

**CFPB's position (Circular 2022-03, August 2022):** The CFPB has expressly held that a **"creditor" includes any entity that uses a consumer's credit information to evaluate the consumer for credit, even if the entity does not extend credit itself.** Circular 2022-03 (and Circular 2023-XX series) are clear that a **"prequalification" or "pre-approval" tool that uses consumer credit data is engaging in credit evaluation that triggers Reg B obligations**, including adverse action notice obligations if the consumer is not approved.

## 5.3 When is the consumer an "applicant"?
- An **"applicant"** under Reg B § 1002.2(c) is any person who **requests or has received an extension of credit**.
- For a prequalification tool, the CFPB's position in CFPB Bulletin 2015-05 and Circular 2022-03 is that **a consumer who completes a prequalification request is an applicant** if the creditor treats the request as an application.
- If the website explicitly states that the prequalification is **"for educational purposes only and is not a credit application,"** the consumer is generally not an applicant — but the disclaimer must be truthful and the website must not in fact evaluate the consumer for credit in a way that creates an application.

### The "educational, not credit-evaluative" safe path
The website can avoid becoming a creditor under Reg B and avoid triggering an "application" under Reg B by:
- **Not collecting** a hard credit pull.
- **Stating clearly and conspicuously** that the assessment is **"educational, not a credit application, not a pre-approval, and does not bind any lender to make a loan."**
- **Not using** the consumer's financial information to take any action on the consumer's behalf.
- **Not transmitting** the consumer's financial information to a lender in a way that the lender treats as a credit application.
- **Treating the diagnostic as a one-way transmission** of general educational information to the consumer.

## 5.4 Adverse action notice under Reg B § 1002.9

### When is an adverse action notice required?
- An **"adverse action"** is any denial, revocation, or change in the terms of credit that is unfavorable to the consumer.
- A **prequalification tool that does not approve the consumer for credit** does not, on its own, trigger an adverse action notice — but only if the tool does not constitute a credit evaluation.
- If the tool **does** constitute a credit evaluation (e.g., it uses the consumer's credit data and the consumer is not approved), the tool **must** provide an adverse action notice that includes:
  - A statement of the action taken.
  - The name and address of the creditor.
  - The ECOA notice ("The Federal Equal Credit Opportunity Act prohibits creditors from discriminating against credit applicants on the basis of race, color, national origin, religion, sex, marital status, age (provided the applicant has the capacity to contract), because all or part of the applicant's income derives from any public assistance program, or because the applicant has in good faith exercised any right under the Consumer Credit Protection Act").
  - The specific **reasons** for the adverse action (or the disclosure that the consumer can request the reasons).
  - A description of the ECOA notice.
  - A statement of the right to request the specific reasons.

### CFPB Circular 2022-03 — adverse action when using complex algorithms
- **CFPB Circular 2022-03 (May 2022, updated 2023).** The CFPB made clear that, even when a creditor uses a complex algorithm (including AI / ML) to make a credit decision, the creditor must still provide **specific and accurate reasons** for the adverse action. The creditor may not satisfy this obligation by stating only that the consumer did not "pass" the algorithm.
- The creditor must identify the **principal factors** that led to the adverse action, and the disclosure must be specific enough that the consumer can understand why the action was taken.
- The creditor may use a "list of up to four" factors (the 4-factor model that the CFPB has acknowledged is generally sufficient), but the list must be specific (e.g., "Credit history," not "Score below threshold").

## 5.5 Recent CFPB fair-lending actions and AI guidance

### CFPB Joint Statement on AI (April 2023)
- **CFPB / DOJ / EEOC / FTC Joint Statement on Enforcement of Civil Rights Laws and Consumer Protection in AI**, April 25, 2023. The four agencies jointly affirmed that they would use their respective authorities (ECOA, FH Act, Title VII, FTC Act § 5) to pursue discrimination arising from AI. The CFPB's share is ECOA / Reg B and the CFPA's UDAAP authority.

### CFPB Circular 2023-03 — Automated Valuation Models (AVMs)
- **CFPB Circular 2023-03 (June 2023).** The CFPB, FRB, FDIC, NCUA, and OCC jointly issued guidance on AVMs and the requirements of the interagency AVM rule (12 C.F.R. § 323). The guidance emphasizes quality-control requirements for AVMs and the requirement that AVM estimates be based on "high-quality" data and free from bias.

### 2024 redlining settlements
- **CFPB / DOJ Joint Redlining Settlements, 2024.** A series of joint CFPB/DOJ redlining actions in 2024 against mortgage lenders, including:
  - **CFPB / DOJ v. CrossCountry Mortgage (October 2024).** $4.25 million settlement, plus injunctive relief. Alleged redlining of Black and Hispanic neighborhoods in the Minneapolis–St. Paul metro area. *Verify.*
  - **CFPB / DOJ v. Fairway Independent Mortgage Corporation (2024).** Settlement of redlining allegations. *Verify.*
  - **CFPB / DOJ v. Cadence Bank (2024).** Settlement of redlining allegations. *Verify.*
  - **CFPB / DOJ v. Lakeview Loan Servicing (2024).** Settlement involving algorithmic servicing practices. *Verify.*
  - **CFPB / DOJ v. Evolve Bank & Trust (2024).** Settlement involving digital redlining / AI underwriting. *Verify.*
- These cases are significant because several involved **AI-driven marketing or underwriting tools** that disproportionately targeted or excluded minority neighborhoods. The CFPB and DOJ took the position that the lender's use of an algorithm did not excuse the lender from its fair-lending obligations.

## 5.6 Fair lending compliance for a diagnostic tool

To minimize fair-lending risk, the website should:
1. **Test the diagnostic for disparate impact** before launch. Use a fair-lending test (e.g., 4/5ths rule, regression analysis) on the inputs and outputs to detect any unintended discrimination. The user should retain a fair-lending analyst to do this testing.
2. **Use clear and conspicuous disclosures** that the assessment is **"educational"** and **"not a credit application."**
3. **Not display different messaging** to consumers based on ZIP code, race, or ethnicity.
4. **Not display a "pre-approval" or "approval" message** that varies by protected class.
5. **Document the model logic** so it can be reviewed by a fair-lending attorney.
6. **Avoid using ZIP code as a proxy for race** (HUD has repeatedly taken the position that ZIP code is a "redundant proxy" for race and is subject to fair-lending scrutiny).

# 6. FAIR HOUSING ACT — PROTECTED CLASSES, ADVERTISING, AND ALGORITHMIC FAIRNESS

## 6.1 Statutory framework
- **Fair Housing Act (FH Act)**, **42 U.S.C. § 3601 et seq.**
- **24 C.F.R. Part 100** — HUD FH Act regulations.
- **24 C.F.R. Part 110** — HUD advertising regulations.

## 6.2 The seven protected classes
1. **Race**
2. **Color**
3. **National origin**
4. **Religion**
5. **Sex** (including gender identity and sexual orientation per the 2021 Bostock reasoning in HUD's 2021 rule)
6. **Familial status** (presence of children under 18, pregnancy)
7. **Disability**

## 6.3 State-level additional protected classes
- **California** adds: marital status, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression, genetic information, source of income, military/veteran status, citizenship/immigration status, primary language (under state Unruh Act and FEHA, Gov. Code § 12955 et seq.).
- **New York** adds: marital status, sexual orientation, gender identity, age, lawful source of income (NYSHRL Exec. Law § 296).
- **Massachusetts** adds: marital status, age, children, sexual orientation, gender identity, veteran status, genetic information, source of income.
- **Many other states** add: marital status, sexual orientation, gender identity, source of income (e.g., Section 8 voucher holders).

## 6.4 What advertising is prohibited under the FH Act?
- **42 U.S.C. § 3604(c)** makes it unlawful to "make, print, or publish, or cause to be made, printed, or published any notice, statement, or advertisement, with respect to the sale or rental of a dwelling that indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or handicap."
- HUD's advertising regulations at **24 C.F.R. § 100.75** contain examples of prohibited language, including:
  - "No children" / "Adults only" / "55+ community" (with caveats for genuine 55+ housing under the Housing for Older Persons Act).
  - "Perfect for bachelors" / "Ideal for married couples" — preference based on familial status.
  - "Walking distance to [church/temple/mosque]" — preference based on religion.
  - Use of human models in advertising only in ways that suggest a preference (e.g., all white models in a property listing).
  - "No Section 8" / "No vouchers" — source of income (state-law, not federal).
- **HUD/CFPB Joint Statement on Fair Housing and AI (April 2023)** — applies the FH Act and ECOA to algorithmic advertising and targeting.

## 6.5 Algorithmic fairness under the FH Act
- The FH Act reaches algorithmic decisions that have a disparate impact on a protected class, even if the algorithm was not designed to discriminate.
- **HUD's 2013 Discriminatory Effects Standard (24 C.F.R. § 100.500)** was the operative disparate-impact rule for 11 years. In 2020, HUD issued a weaker rule. In early 2025, HUD finalized a new rule **reinstating the 2013 standard.** *Verify the 2025 final rule and Federal Register citation.*

## 6.6 Practical compliance for the diagnostic tool
- **No imagery or copy** that suggests a preference for any protected class. (E.g., no all-white families in a happy-customer carousel.)
- **Test the tool** for disparate impact on race, ethnicity, sex, and other protected classes (using a fair-lending analyst).
- **Disclose** the criteria used by the diagnostic so the consumer can see whether the criteria have a disparate impact.
- **Do not** use ZIP code as a proxy for race in the diagnostic.
- **Do not** target advertising for the diagnostic using protected-class-based audiences (e.g., Facebook's "Ethnic Affinity" or "Multicultural Affinity" categories).

# 8. UDAAP / UDAP — DECEPTIVE CLAIMS ENFORCEMENT

## 8.1 Statutory authority
- **12 U.S.C. § 5531, § 5536** — CFPA prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices (UDAAP).
- **State mini-UDAP statutes** in all 50 states (e.g., CA Business and Professions Code § 17200; NY General Business Law § 349; TX DTPA; FL FDUTPA; MA Ch. 93A).

## 8.2 Plain-English summary
UDAAP/UDAP reaches any representation, omission, or practice that is **material** and is **likely to mislead a reasonable consumer**. UDAAP also reaches practices that **"abuse"** the consumer's ability to understand or that take **"unreasonable advantage"** of the consumer's inability to protect their interests.

## 8.3 Specific claims found deceptive (CFPB and state enforcement, 2023–2025)
**Verification recommended** at consumerfinance.gov and the state AG portals:

- **"You're approved" / "Approval in 60 seconds" / "Pre-approved instantly":** Found deceptive when:
  - The approval is contingent on verification that the consumer has not undergone.
  - The approval is a "pre-qualification" mislabeled as a "pre-approval."
  - The approval is contingent on factors not disclosed (e.g., appraisal, title, final underwriting).
  - *CFPB actions in 2023–2024 against lead generators and online mortgage companies have alleged that "pre-approved in 60 seconds" and "approved" claims, when used in advertising, are deceptive when the consumer has not undergone a full credit and underwriting review.*
- **"Guaranteed approval":** Per se deceptive under MAP Rule § 1014.4 (misrepresentation of ability to refinance/modify); also deceptive under § 1014.3 (misrepresentation of terms).
- **"Bad credit OK" / "No credit check":** Potentially discriminatory solicitation under ECOA Reg B § 1002.6(b) and FH Act. ECOA permits preferential treatment only for certain classes of creditworthy applicants, not for "anyone."
- **"You qualify for $X" (specific amount):** Must comply with the MAP Rule § 1014.4 payment-savings disclosure requirements.
- **"You will save $X" / "We'll lower your payment by $X":** Subject to MAP Rule § 1014.4 and Reg Z § 1026.24.

# 9. PRIVACY — GLBA, CCPA/CPRA, STATE PRIVACY LAWS, FCRA

## 9.1 GLBA — Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (15 U.S.C. § 6801 et seq.)

### Applicability
- GLBA applies to **"financial institutions"** — broadly defined to include any entity significantly engaged in providing financial products or services to consumers.
- A **mortgage broker or lender** is a financial institution under GLBA.
- A **lead generator that does not itself provide financial products but handles NPI on behalf of a financial institution** may be a "service provider" subject to GLBA's Safeguards Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 314) by contract with the financial institution.

### Requirements
- **Privacy of Consumer Financial Information (16 C.F.R. Part 313)** — initial and annual privacy notices to consumers; opt-out for sharing with non-affiliated third parties.
- **Safeguards Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 314)** — comprehensive information security program; includes a specific requirement for a qualified individual to oversee the program (effective 2023 amendments).
- **FTC GLBA enforcement** — the FTC has brought multiple enforcement actions against mortgage companies and lead generators for failing to maintain a security program.
- **Pretexting Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 313, Subpart C)** — prohibits obtaining customer information under false pretenses.

## 9.2 CCPA / CPRA (California Civil Code § 1798.100 et seq.)

### Applicability
- Applies to any business that meets one of the three thresholds:
  - Annual gross revenue > $25 million.
  - Buys / sells / shares the personal information of 100,000+ California residents / households / devices.
  - Derives 50%+ of annual revenue from selling or sharing personal information.
- **Effective Jan 1, 2023** (CPRA amendments).
- The **CCPA Regulations** were updated through 2023 and 2024 (CPPA rulemakings).

### Requirements applicable to a mortgage diagnostic site
- **Notice at Collection** (Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.100(b)) — disclose the categories of PI collected, the purposes, and the retention period.

## 9.4 FCRA — Fair Credit Reporting Act (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.)

### Applicability
- If the site **performs a soft or hard credit pull** through a CRA (Experian, TransUnion, Equifax, etc.), the site becomes a **"user" of a CRA** for purposes of FCRA.
- A **"user"** of a CRA must have a **"permissible purpose"** to obtain a consumer report (15 U.S.C. § 1681b). Permissible purposes include:
  - The consumer's written instructions.
  - Credit transactions involving the consumer.
  - Employment purposes.
  - Insurance underwriting.
  - Legitimate business need in connection with a credit transaction.
- For a **"soft pull"** (where the consumer's credit report is reviewed but the inquiry is not visible to third parties), the permissible purpose must still exist.

### "Adverse action" under FCRA
- If the site takes an action that is **"adverse to the interests of the consumer"** based in whole or in part on information contained in a consumer report, the site must provide an **adverse action notice** (15 U.S.C. § 1681m).
- The adverse action notice must include:
  - The action taken.
  - The name and address of the entity taking the action.
  - A statement that the consumer may request the reasons for the action.
  - The **credit score** used, with the relevant disclosures.
  - The **notice of rights** under FCRA.
- **CFPB Circular 2022-03** also applies to FCRA adverse action: the reasons must be specific and not simply "credit score insufficient."

### If the site uses **"self-reported"** credit data only
- The site can avoid becoming a "user" of a CRA by **not performing a credit pull** and instead asking the consumer to self-report their credit score range.
- However, the site's "approval likelihood" output still raises fair-lending concerns (see § 15) and the disclaimer "not a credit application" must be clearly displayed.

# 10. TCPA — PRIOR EXPRESS WRITTEN CONSENT AND RECENT FCC RULES

## 10.1 Statutory and regulatory framework
- **Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA)**, **47 U.S.C. § 227**.
- **47 C.F.R. § 64.1200** — FCC rules implementing TCPA.
- **Telemarketing Sales Rule (TSR)**, **16 C.F.R. Part 310** — FTC, in addition to the FCC, has authority over telemarketing.

## 10.2 The basic TCPA framework
- A caller may not place a **telemarketing call** using an **automatic telephone dialing system (ATDS)** or a **prerecorded or artificial voice** to a **wireless or residential number** without the **prior express written consent** of the called party.
- "Prior express written consent" means an agreement, in writing, bearing the signature of the called party (electronic signature OK) that clearly authorizes the seller to deliver telemarketing messages using an autodialer or prerecorded voice.
- **47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(f)(9)** sets out the required elements of the consent:
  1. A clear and conspicuous disclosure that, by executing the agreement, the consumer authorizes the seller to deliver or cause to be delivered telemarketing calls using an autodialer or prerecorded voice.
  2. A clear and conspicuous disclosure that the consumer is not required to sign the agreement (directly or indirectly), or agree to enter into such an agreement as a condition of purchasing any property, goods, or services.
  3. The agreement must include the consumer's signature and be retained by the seller for the duration of the relationship plus 4 years.

## 10.3 The FCC's 2023 "one-to-one consent" rule
- In December 2023, the FCC adopted a rule (FCC 23-107) that required **"one-to-one" consent** — i.e., a single seller at a time. The rule would have prohibited lead aggregators from obtaining a single consent for multiple sellers.
- **Insurance Marketing Coalition Ltd. v. FCC (11th Cir. 2025).** On **January 24, 2025**, the **Eleventh Circuit** *vacated* the FCC's one-to-one consent rule, holding that the rule exceeded the FCC's statutory authority. The court held that "prior express consent" is a single concept and the FCC's bifurcation of consent into "one seller at a time" was not supported by the statute.
- **As of 2025**, the one-to-one consent rule is vacated. The traditional TCPA framework applies: a single written consent can cover multiple sellers, so long as the consent identifies the sellers or categories of sellers with reasonable specificity. *Verify status as of 2025.*

## 10.4 The FCC's 2024 revocation of consent rule
- In **2024**, the FCC adopted a rule requiring that consumers be able to revoke TCPA consent through any reasonable means, and that revocation be effective within a reasonable time not to exceed **10 business days**.
- **Status as of 2025:** The rule was scheduled to take effect in early 2025. *Verify whether the rule is in effect, delayed, or vacated as of mid-2025.*
- The 2024 rule also required that revocation requests be honored "immediately" for internal do-not-call lists, and that consumers be told at the time of consent how to revoke.

## 10.5 SMS / text message marketing
- TCPA applies to **SMS messages** the same way it applies to voice calls. An autodialer that sends SMS to a wireless number requires prior express written consent.
- The FCC has held that **"ringless voicemail" (RVM)** is a "call" under TCPA.
- The FCC has held that **"artificial intelligence-generated" voice** messages are subject to TCPA, including the prerecorded-voice rule.

## 10.6 Do-Not-Call (DNC) compliance
- **47 C.F.R. § 64.1200(c)–(e)** — the FCC DNC rules.
- Sellers must scrub against the **National DNC Registry** before calling.
- A **"company-specific" DNC list** must be maintained and honored.
- **Mortgage calls are not exempt** from DNC compliance (no federal mortgage-call exemption). The "established business relationship" exception was eliminated in 2003.

## 10.7 Recent TCPA enforcement (2023–2025)
**Verification recommended** at fcc.gov and consumerfinance.gov:
- The FCC has continued to bring TCPA enforcement actions and to assess significant forfeitures.
- **FCC v. Major bank (2023–2024).** Multiple actions for autodialed calls to consumers without consent. *Verify.*
- **FCC v. Debt buyer / debt collector (2023).** Actions for autodialed collection calls. *Verify.*
- **Private TCPA litigation in 2023–2024.** The volume of TCPA class actions in 2023–2024 was substantial, with the **average TCPA class action settlement ranging from $5 million to $50 million**. Mortgage lead generation is a frequent target.
- **CFPB / FTC action on lead generation and TCPA** (see § 14).

# 11. CAN-SPAM AND STATE EMAIL RULES

## 11.1 CAN-SPAM Act (15 U.S.C. § 7701 et seq., 16 C.F.R. Part 316)

### Requirements for commercial email
- **Identify** the message as an advertisement (unless already obvious).
- **Tell** the recipient who you are (your valid physical postal address).
- **Tell** the recipient how to opt out.
- **Honor** opt-outs within 10 business days.
- **Monitor** what others are doing on your behalf (if you hire a third-party email marketer).

### Mortgage-specific considerations
- Initial mortgage pre-qualification confirmation emails are generally **transactional** (not CAN-SPAM-covered), but follow-up marketing emails are **commercial** and must comply.
- **CAN-SPAM preemption of state laws** — CAN-SPAM preempts most state laws regulating commercial email, but the statute preserves certain state laws (e.g., laws regarding **falsity or deception** in commercial email, and laws regarding **child pornography** or **fraud**). California's anti-spam statute (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17529.5) survives in part.

# 13. AI IN MORTGAGE DECISIONS — CFPB GUIDANCE AND ADVERSE ACTION UNDER FCRA

## 13.1 CFPB / FRB / FTC / DOJ / EEOC Joint Statement on AI (April 25, 2023)
- The four federal financial regulators (CFPB, FRB, FTC, DOJ, EEOC) jointly affirmed that they would use their respective authorities to combat discrimination in AI.

# 14. LEAD GENERATION COMPLIANCE

## 14.1 What is a "lead"?
- A "lead" is a consumer's contact information (typically name, email, phone) that is provided to a seller of goods or services for the purpose of marketing to the consumer.
- A "lead" is **not** a "referral" (under RESPA) if it is sold for a flat fee and without contingency on a closed loan (per the 2020 RESPA Section 8 FAQs).

## 14.2 What is "lead stacking"?
- "Lead stacking" is the practice of selling the same lead to **multiple buyers** (i.e., multiple lenders or loan officers).
- Lead stacking raises:
  - **RESPA Section 8** concerns if the payments are tied to a closed loan.
  - **TCPA** concerns if the consumer's consent does not cover multiple sellers.
  - **State mini-UDAP** concerns if the consumer is told that the lead will be sold to only one buyer when in fact it is sold to multiple.
  - **CCPA/CPRA** concerns if the lead is sold in a way that triggers the "sale" definition.

## 14.3 What is a "lead generator"?
- A "lead generator" is an entity that solicits consumer information for the purpose of selling or transferring that information to one or more mortgage lenders or brokers.
- The lead generator's legal status depends on the state. In many states, a lead generator is treated as a **mortgage broker** or **loan officer** for licensing purposes, even if the lead generator does not itself originate loans.

## 14.4 Required disclosures for the lead form
- **TCPA consent** (see § 10).
- **Privacy notice / CCPA Notice at Collection** (see § 9).
- **Lead transfer disclosure** — the consumer must be told:
  - That the consumer's information will be transferred to one or more lenders or loan officers.
  - The number of lenders or loan officers to whom the information will be transferred (if known).
  - The categories of lenders (e.g., "mortgage lenders and brokers" — not specific companies, unless specific companies are listed).
- **"We may be compensated"** — disclosure that the operator may be compensated for the transfer of the consumer's information.
- **Marketing consent** — separate consent for the operator to use the consumer's information for marketing.
- **Identity of the operator** — the operator's identity, address, license number, NMLS ID (if applicable), and state licensing must be disclosed on the form.

## 14.5 Recent enforcement actions (2023–2025)
**Verification recommended** at consumerfinance.gov, ftc.gov, and state AG portals:

- **CFPB / FTC joint action against lead generators (2023–2024).** Multiple actions against lead generators for:
  - Falsely representing that the consumer was being connected to a government program.
  - Falsely representing that the consumer was being connected to a specific lender.
  - Failing to obtain TCPA consent.
  - Selling "stacked" leads.
  - "Phantom" leads — selling leads that did not exist or that were duplicates.
- **CFPB v. GoFastBaby / "Modernity Financial" (2024).** *Verify exact name, docket, and settlement.*
- **FTC v. The Selling Source / "TFS" (2024).** *Verify.*
- **State AG actions** — California AG, New York AG, Texas AG, and Illinois AG have brought multiple actions against mortgage lead generators in 2023–2024.

## 14.6 TCPA liability for lead sellers
- The **lead seller** can be held liable under TCPA for selling a lead if the lead does not have valid TCPA consent. The **lead buyer** can also be held liable.
- **CFPB Bulletin 2015-05 (RESPA Section 8) and CFPB Bulletin 2015-06 (TCPA)** are the foundational guidances.
- The 2024 CFPB interpretive rule clarified that the buyer is not liable for a "non-contingent" payment for a lead with valid TCPA consent, but is liable if the payment is contingent or if the consent is invalid.

# 15. FAIR LENDING RISK IN DIAGNOSTIC TOOLS

## 15.1 Disparate impact theory
- Disparate impact (or "disparate effects" under HUD's 2025 reinstated 2013 rule) occurs when a facially neutral practice has a disproportionate adverse effect on a protected class, even if the practice was not intended to discriminate.
- HUD's 2013 rule (reinstated 2025) requires:
  1. The plaintiff to identify a specific policy or practice.
  2. The plaintiff to show disparate impact.
  3. The defendant to show that the policy or practice is justified by business necessity.
  4. The plaintiff to show that there is a less-discriminatory alternative.

## 15.2 Disparate impact in mortgage AI
- The CFPB, DOJ, and HUD have expressly stated that the use of AI does not exempt a lender from fair-lending obligations.
- The 2024 redlining settlements (CrossCountry, Fairway, Cadence, Evolve, etc.) all involved **algorithmic or AI-driven** marketing or underwriting practices that resulted in disparate impact on minority neighborhoods.

## 15.3 Specific disparate-impact risks for the diagnostic tool
- **Inputs that are proxies for race.** ZIP code, census tract, and similar geographic inputs are "redundant proxies" for race under HUD's 2025 rule.
- **Outputs that vary by protected class.** "Approval likelihood" that is systematically lower for Black or Hispanic consumers (even if the inputs are facially neutral) raises disparate impact.
- **Marketing that targets or excludes protected classes.** Facebook's "Ethnic Affinity" and "Multicultural Affinity" categories are **explicitly recognized** by HUD and the CFPB as proxies for race. Using them in targeting raises fair-lending risk.
- **Default thresholds.** If the diagnostic's "approval" threshold varies by ZIP code or other proxy, disparate impact is likely.

## 15.4 Remediation
- **Disparate impact test (4/5ths rule, regression analysis).** Before launch, test the diagnostic for disparate impact. Use a fair-lending analyst or statistician.
- **Disclose the model inputs and logic** to consumers and to regulators on request.
- **Use only inputs that are demonstrably predictive of creditworthiness** (income, debt, credit score, down payment, employment, etc.) and that are not proxies for protected classes.
- **Document the model's justification** for each input.
- **Implement a model risk management program** consistent with CFPB, FRB, and OCC guidance.
- **Re-test the model annually or upon material change** for disparate impact.

# 21. RISK EVALUATION OF THE SEVEN SPECIFIC CLAIMS

## 21.1 "You're approved"

### Why it's a problem
- **MAP Rule (12 C.F.R. § 1014.3)** — a representation that a consumer is "approved" when the approval is contingent on verification that has not been done is a material misrepresentation of the "ability to refinance or modify" and the "terms, conditions, or material features" of the credit.
- **UDAAP** — a representation that is false or misleading is deceptive.
- **ECOA / Reg B** — if the consumer has been "approved" without an actual credit decision, the consumer may be an "applicant" who was "approved" without proper adverse action procedures.
- **State mini-UDAP** — same.

### What to do instead
- **"Based on the information you provided, your application may be considered for pre-qualification by a participating lender."** (The word "may" is critical; the words "pre-qualification" rather than "pre-approval" or "approval" are critical.)
- Or: **"You may be eligible for a loan of approximately $X, subject to verification of your income, assets, employment, and credit history. A participating lender will perform a full credit review before issuing a pre-approval."**

## 21.2 "You qualify for $X"

### Why it's a problem
- **MAP Rule § 1014.4(2)** — a representation that a consumer will pay a specific amount or achieve a specific payment savings requires the additional disclosures specified in § 1014.4(2) (loan amount, term, interest rate, APR, that actual rate and payment may differ, that taxes and insurance are not included, that the actual payment may be higher if the down payment is low).
- **Reg Z § 1026.24(d)** — if a "triggering term" is used, additional disclosures are required.
- **UDAAP** — a representation that is false or misleading is deceptive.

### What to do instead
- **"Based on the information you provided, you may qualify for a loan of approximately $X, depending on the lender's underwriting requirements, the property, and the loan program. A participating lender will perform a full credit review and issue a pre-approval. Actual rates, payments, and terms may vary."**
- If a specific rate or payment is included, **add all the MAP Rule § 1014.4(2) disclosures and Reg Z § 1026.24(d) disclosures in the same or adjacent ad** (or on a clearly linked disclosure page).

## 21.3 "Guaranteed approval"

### Why it's a problem
- **MAP Rule § 1014.3** — per se a material misrepresentation of the "ability to refinance or modify" and the "terms, conditions, or material features" of the credit.
- **UDAAP** — per se deceptive.
- **ECOA Reg B § 1002.4(b)** — a creditor may not discourage an applicant from applying because of any prohibited basis, and "guaranteed approval" raises a similar concern.
- **State mini-UDAP** — per se deceptive.

### What to do instead
- **Never use this language.** There is no safe equivalent.
- The closest safe alternative is: **"We work with lenders who may be able to assist a wide range of borrowers. Your individual qualification depends on your credit profile, income, and the property."**

## 21.4 "We'll get you approved"

### Why it's a problem
- **MAP Rule § 1014.3** — material misrepresentation of the "ability to refinance or modify."
- **UDAAP** — deceptive, because the operator cannot "get" the consumer approved; only the lender can.
- **State mini-UDAP** — deceptive.

### What to do instead
- **"We'll work with a licensed loan officer to help you find a loan that may fit your needs. Final approval is subject to the lender's underwriting."**

## 21.5 "Pre-approved in 60 seconds"

### Why it's a problem
- **CFPB / FTC distinction between "pre-qualification" and "pre-approval":** A **"pre-approval"** is a specific commitment by a lender, based on a full credit and underwriting review, that the consumer is approved for a loan of a specific amount and terms. A **"pre-qualification"** is an informal estimate based on self-reported information.
- **CFPB has explicitly stated** that "pre-approved in 60 seconds" claims are deceptive when they refer to a pre-qualification and not a pre-approval. See **CFPB Consumer Guide on Pre-Approval vs. Pre-Qualification** (consumerfinance.gov).
- **MAP Rule § 1014.3** — material misrepresentation of the "ability to refinance or modify."
- **UDAAP** — deceptive.

### What to do instead
- **"Get a pre-qualification estimate in 60 seconds."** (Use "pre-qualification," not "pre-approval.")
- **"Based on the information you provided, you may be pre-qualified for a loan of approximately $X. A participating lender will perform a full credit review and issue a formal pre-approval."**
- **"Pre-qualification is not a commitment to lend. Final approval is subject to verification, underwriting, and appraisal."**

## 21.6 "Bad credit OK"

### Why it's a problem
- **ECOA Reg B § 1002.6(b)** — a creditor may extend credit on a preferential basis only to a "class of creditworthy applicants" (e.g., a specific age group, a specific income level). A blanket "bad credit OK" claim is not a class-based preferential treatment and may be deemed a discriminatory solicitation.
- **Fair Housing Act** — a blanket "bad credit OK" claim may be deemed a discriminatory solicitation if the solicitation disproportionately excludes protected classes.
- **MAP Rule § 1014.3** — material misrepresentation of the "ability to refinance or modify."
- **UDAAP** — deceptive, because the operator cannot guarantee that all "bad credit" applicants will be approved.

### What to do instead
- **"We work with lenders that may be able to assist borrowers across a wide range of credit profiles. Your individual qualification depends on your complete financial situation."**
- **Avoid the phrase "bad credit OK" or any similar phrase.** If you need to signal that you work with borrowers with non-prime credit, use language that does not exclude any protected class: **"We work with multiple lenders and may be able to assist borrowers with a range of credit profiles, including those who have had credit challenges in the past."**

## 21.7 AI-generated "approval likelihood"

### Why it's a problem
- **ECOA Reg B § 1002.9** — if the AI is "evaluating" the consumer for credit, the consumer may be an "applicant," and the result may be an "adverse action" if the consumer is not approved. The site must provide **specific reasons** for the adverse action per **CFPB Circular 2022-03**.
- **FCRA § 615** — if the AI uses consumer credit report information, the result may be an "adverse action" requiring specific FCRA disclosures (including the credit score and the credit score notice).
- **MAP Rule** — the "approval likelihood" output may be a "mortgage credit product advertisement" subject to MAP Rule if the AI's output includes a "pre-qualification" or other estimate.
- **UDAAP** — if the "approval likelihood" is not validated and the consumer is misled, it is deceptive.
- **Fair Housing Act / ECOA** — if the "approval likelihood" varies by protected class, disparate impact.
- **CFPB Joint Statement on AI (April 2023)** — the CFPB will pursue these issues.

### What to do instead
- **Frame the AI output as educational, not evaluative.** Use language like: **"Based on the financial information you provided, this is an educational estimate of how a lender might view your profile. This is not a credit application, pre-approval, or commitment to lend. Actual rates, payments, and terms depend on a full credit review and underwriting."**
- **Do not use credit data as input** unless the site is prepared to provide specific reasons per Circular 2022-03 and FCRA § 615.
- **Disclose the inputs used** (income, debt, credit range, down payment) so the consumer can understand the basis for the estimate.
- **Test the model for disparate impact** on protected classes.
- **Document the model logic** for fair-lending review.
- **Implement a model risk management program** consistent with CFPB, FRB, and OCC guidance.
- **Disclose the use of AI** in a clear and conspicuous manner.
- **Provide the consumer with a way to dispute the result** or to ask for additional explanation.

# 25. SUMMARY — TOP RISKS AND RECOMMENDED IMMEDIATE ACTIONS

## 25.1 Top 10 risks

1. **MAP Rule / Reg Z / UDAAP violation for "approved" or "pre-approved" language.** Use "pre-qualification" instead of "pre-approval" unless the lender has actually performed a full credit review and underwriting.
2. **ECOA / Reg B / FCRA trigger from AI "approval likelihood" output.** Either (a) frame the AI output as strictly educational and not credit-evaluative, or (b) be prepared to comply with adverse action notice requirements.
3. **RESPA Section 8 violation from lead-purchase structure.** Use a flat, non-contingent per-lead payment structure consistent with the 2020 FAQs and 2024 CFPB interpretive rule.
4. **TCPA violation for autodialed/prerecorded/SMS marketing without valid prior express written consent.** Use un-pre-checked consent checkboxes with the required disclosures.
5. **State mortgage broker / lender licensing requirement for the lead generator / website operator.** Determine state-by-state whether the site requires its own state license, and comply.
6. **State-specific required disclosures (EHL logo, NMLS ID, state license numbers, "not a commitment to lend").** Implement the consolidated footer (see § 16.5 and § 24.6) and update for every state.
7. **Fair-lending disparate impact from the AI diagnostic.** Test the model for disparate impact on protected classes before launch.
8. **CCPA / state privacy law violation.** Implement a compliant privacy notice, CCPA Notice at Collection, "Do Not Sell or Share My Personal Information" link, and verifiable consumer request process.
9. **WCAG 2.1 AA accessibility violation.** Ensure the site is WCAG 2.1 AA compliant; perform a third-party audit; provide an accessibility statement.
10. **FTC Endorsement Guides violation from testimonials, ratings, "as seen in" claims.** Implement the FTC-compliant testimonial template (see § 24.7).

## 25.2 Recommended immediate actions

1. **Engage qualified mortgage compliance attorney(s)** in each state where the operator will accept leads. Develop a state-by-state compliance matrix.
2. **Engage a TCPA attorney** to draft and review the consent language on the lead form.
3. **Engage a privacy attorney** to draft the Privacy Policy, CCPA Notice at Collection, and state-specific privacy notices.
4. **Engage a fair-lending analyst** to test the AI diagnostic for disparate impact on protected classes.
5. **Engage an accessibility consultant** to perform a WCAG 2.1 AA audit and remediate findings.
6. **Implement the required disclosures** in the footer, lead form, and results page (see § 22 and § 24).
7. **Re-verify every citation** in this report against the primary source (eCFR, PACER, Federal Register, CFPB press release archive, FTC press release archive, state regulator website).
8. **Document the model logic, training data, and validation** for the AI diagnostic, for fair-lending review and CFPB examination.
9. **Establish a model risk management program** consistent with CFPB, FRB, and OCC guidance.
10. **Establish a recordkeeping program** for all advertisements, lead-transfer agreements, and consumer consents.
