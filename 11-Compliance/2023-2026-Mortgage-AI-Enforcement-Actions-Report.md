# U.S. Mortgage & AI Enforcement Actions 2023–2026
## Primary-Source Research Report — CFPB, FTC, HUD/DOJ, and State Regulators

**Subject:** Recent (2023 through 2026) federal and state enforcement actions, guidance, and rulemaking directed at mortgage lead generation, AI-driven mortgage tools, mortgage advertising, and pre-qualification/pre-approval claims, with a focus on Rocket Mortgage, UWM, loanDepot, Fairway, the MAP Rule, RESPA Section 8, the SAFE Act / NMLS unique identifier, the joint CFPB/DOJ redlining initiative, and state AI laws (Colorado AI Act, California SB 1047 / AB 2013, NYC LL 144, Illinois AI Video Interview Act).

**Prepared:** August 2026

> ⚠️ **Methodology note.** This report draws on (i) primary documents fetched directly from `consumerfinance.gov` (CFPB enforcement pages, newsroom archive, files.consumerfinance.gov, and the Federal Register), (ii) primary documents fetched from `ftc.gov` (press releases, cases, Federal Register, and FTC Final Rules), (iii) primary documents from the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) press release RSS feed (`justice.gov/news/rss`), (iv) HUD and Federal Register pages, and (v) state regulator pages (CA DFPI, NY DFS, MA, IL, CO, etc.). A few case-specific DOJ/HUD press releases were not directly retrievable during this research session because the relevant URLs were either rate-limited or behind an interstitial challenge. Where this is the case, the report cites the case name, docket number, date, and press-release title, and notes that the URL was not directly retrievable. **Every citation should be re-verified against eCFR, PACER, the relevant regulator's press release archive, and the agency's own guidance portal before being relied on for any filing, contract, marketing claim, or policy decision on a live site.** The structure of the report makes that verification mechanical (each claim is keyed to a citation that can be looked up).

## 1.1 Rocket Homes Real Estate LLC; JMG Holding Partners LLC d/b/a The Jason Mitchell Group; 45 affiliates; Jason Mitchell

- **Case name / docket:** *CFPB v. Rocket Homes Real Estate LLC, et al.*, No. 2:24-cv-13442 (E.D. Mich., filed Dec. 23, 2024; dismissed Feb. 28, 2025).
- **Regulator:** CFPB.
- **Statute / rule:** Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA), 12 U.S.C. § 2607, and Regulation X, 12 C.F.R. Part 1024.
- **Prohibited conduct alleged:** The complaint alleged that Rocket Homes and the Jason Mitchell Group of real-estate brokerages violated RESPA Section 8 by entering into an illegal kickback scheme to steer borrowers to Rocket Mortgage. The CFPB alleged that Rocket Homes provided "things of value" to the Jason Mitchell Group — including payments disguised as advertising and referrals — in exchange for the steering of mortgage-loan referrals.
- **Resolution:** On Feb. 27, 2025, the CFPB filed a notice of dismissal voluntarily dismissing the action against all defendants with prejudice, and on Feb. 28, 2025, the court dismissed the case with prejudice.
- **Settlement amount / penalty:** None — voluntary dismissal.
- **Primary-source URLs:**
  - CFPB enforcement-action page: `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/enforcement/actions/rocket-homes-real-estate-llc-et-al/`
  - Press release: "CFPB Files Lawsuit to Stop Illegal Kickback Scheme to Steer Borrowers to Rocket Mortgage," Dec. 23, 2024, `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-files-lawsuit-stop-illegal-kickback-scheme-steer-borrowers-rocket-mortgage/` (currently archived).
- **Caveat:** The CFPB dismissal with prejudice in early 2025 followed the change in administration and should be read against the backdrop of the April 11, 2025 Acting-Director memo and the May 12, 2025 withdrawal of CFPB guidance. The underlying factual record is preserved in the Dec. 23, 2024 complaint.

## 1.2 Fairway Independent Mortgage Corporation (joint CFPB/DOJ action)

- **Case name / docket:** *CFPB and United States v. Fairway Independent Mortgage Corporation*, No. 2:24-cv-01405 (N.D. Ala., filed Oct. 15, 2024; consent order entered Dec. 3, 2024).
- **Regulators:** CFPB + DOJ (Civil Rights Division, Housing and Civil Enforcement Section; U.S. Attorney's Office for the Northern District of Alabama).
- **Statutes / rules:** Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA), 15 U.S.C. § 1691 *et seq.*, and Regulation B, 12 C.F.R. Part 1002; Consumer Financial Protection Act of 2010 (CFPA), 12 U.S.C. § 5536 (prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices); Fair Housing Act (FHA), 42 U.S.C. § 3601 *et seq.*
- **Prohibited conduct alleged:** Fairway redlined majority-Black neighborhoods in the Birmingham-Hoover Metropolitan Statistical Area (Birmingham MSA). From 2015 through 2022, Fairway operated three retail loan offices and three loan production desks, **all located in majority-white areas**; relied on referrals from real-estate professionals and others, the vast majority of which were located in majority-white areas; and predominantly directed its marketing (including direct mail) to majority-white areas. Only 3.7% of Fairway's applications from 2018 through 2022 were for properties in majority-Black areas, vs. 12.2% for Fairway's peer lenders. The complaint alleged that Fairway's own data showed that it was failing to serve majority-Black neighborhoods, but that prior to October 2022 Fairway took no steps to address redlining risk other than telling loan officers not to discriminate, and had no written marketing or growth plan to address the concern.
- **Settlement / penalty:**
  - **$1.9 million civil penalty** to the CFPB's Civil Penalty Fund.
  - **$7 million for a loan-subsidy program** offering home-purchase, refinance, and home-improvement loans on a more affordable basis in majority-Black neighborhoods in the Birmingham MSA.
  - **At least $1 million to open or acquire a new loan-production office or full-service retail office in a majority-Black neighborhood** in the Birmingham MSA.
  - **At least $500,000 for advertising and outreach** to majority-Black neighborhoods.
  - **At least $250,000 on consumer financial education.**
  - **At least $250,000 on partnerships** with community-based or governmental organizations.
- **Settlement order date:** Court entered the consent order on Dec. 3, 2024.
- **Primary-source URLs:**
  - CFPB enforcement-action page: `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/enforcement/actions/fairway-independent-mortgage-corporation/`
  - Press release: "CFPB and Justice Department Take Action Against Fairway for Redlining Black Neighborhoods in Birmingham, Alabama," Oct. 15, 2024, `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-and-justice-department-take-action-against-fairway-for-redlining-black-neighborhoods-in-birmingham-alabama/` (currently archived).

## 1.3 Townstone Financial, Inc. and Barry Sturner

- **Case name / docket:** *CFPB v. Townstone Financial, Inc. and Barry Sturner*, No. 1:20-cv-04176 (N.D. Ill., filed July 15, 2020; amended complaint Nov. 25, 2020; stipulated final judgment Nov. 7, 2024).
- **Regulator:** CFPB.
- **Statutes / rules:** ECOA, 15 U.S.C. § 1691 *et seq.*; Regulation B, 12 C.F.R. Part 1002; CFPA, 12 U.S.C. § 5536.
- **Prohibited conduct alleged:** The original complaint alleged that Townstone, a non-bank retail-mortgage creditor and broker based in Chicago, engaged in redlining and discouraged applications from residents of majority-Black and majority-Hispanic neighborhoods in the Chicago MSA through its marketing, business-location choices, and statements by its owner. The amended complaint added Barry Sturner (Townstone's sole owner) as a defendant and alleged that Sturner was the fraudulent transferee of more than $2.4 million transferred from Townstone.
- **Resolution:** On Nov. 7, 2024, the court entered a stipulated final judgment and order against Townstone, imposing injunctive relief and a civil money penalty; the Bureau's claim against Sturner was dismissed the same day pursuant to the parties' stipulation. On March 26, 2025, the parties **jointly moved the court to vacate** the stipulated final judgment and order on the grounds of significant undisclosed problems with the Bureau's treatment of the case, "resulting in unmerited investigation and litigation and the infringement of the defendants' First Amendment rights." The court denied the motion on June 12, 2025. On Dec. 3, 2025, the CFPB posted the Vacate filings.
- **Settlement amount / penalty:** Stipulated final judgment (terms in the Nov. 7, 2024 order); subsequently, the parties jointly moved to vacate. (Specific penalty amount available in the consent order at the URL below.)
- **Primary-source URLs:**
  - CFPB enforcement-action page: `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/enforcement/actions/townstone-financial-inc-and-barry-sturner/`
  - Press release: "CFPB Seeks to Vacate Abusive, Unjust Case Against Townstone," March 26, 2025, `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-seeks-to-vacate-abusive-unjust-case-against-townstone/` (currently archived).

- **Statutes / rules:** ECOA, 15 U.S.C. § 1691 *et seq.*; Regulation B, 12 C.F.R. Part 1002; CFPA, 12 U.S.C. § 5536.
- **Prohibited conduct alleged:** The complaint alleged that Draper & Kramer, a non-depository mortgage lender based in Downers Grove, Illinois, engaged in discriminatory mortgage-lending practices in violation of ECOA, Regulation B, and the CFPA.

## 1.7 New Day Financial, LLC (NewDay USA)

- **Case name / docket:** *In re New Day Financial, LLC*, CFPB No. 2024-CFPB-0008 (filed Aug. 29, 2024).
- **Regulator:** CFPB.
- **Statutes / rules:** CFPA, 12 U.S.C. § 5536 (UDAAP).
- **Prohibited conduct found:** NewDay USA is a non-bank direct mortgage lender headquartered in West Palm Beach, Florida, that specializes in offering refinance mortgage loans guaranteed by the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs. Since at least 2015, NewDay USA has provided cash-out refinance loans to consumers, including veterans and active-duty members, many of whom had lower than average credit scores and carry consumer debt. Certain states in which NewDay USA offered its cash-out refinances (North Carolina, Maine, and Minnesota, with different end dates) required lenders to complete a borrower "net benefit" analysis worksheet showing the financial benefit of a refinance to a borrower. The CFPB found that NewDay USA consistently misstated in those worksheets how the consumer's "previous" monthly mortgage payment would compare to the "new" monthly mortgage payment after refinancing with NewDay USA.
- **Resolution:** Consent order, Aug. 29, 2024.
- **Settlement amount / penalty:** **$2.25 million civil money penalty.**
- **Primary-source URLs:**
  - CFPB enforcement-action page: `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/enforcement/actions/new-day-financial-2024/`
  - Press release: "CFPB Orders NewDay USA to Pay $2.25 Million for Illegally Luring Veterans and Military Families into Cash-Out Refinance Loans," Aug. 29, 2024, `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-orders-newday-usa-to-pay-2-25-million-for-illegally-luring-veterans-and-military-families-into-cash-out-refinance-loans/` (currently archived).

## 1.9 RMK Financial Corp. d/b/a Majestic Home Loan (MHL)

- **Case name / docket:** *In re RMK Financial Corp.* (consent order Feb. 27, 2023).
- **Regulator:** CFPB.
- **Statutes / rules:** CFPA, 12 U.S.C. § 5536 (UDAAP); **MAP Rule (Regulation N), 12 C.F.R. Part 1014**; **Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and Regulation Z, 12 C.F.R. § 1026.24** (advertising); and the **April 8, 2015 prior CFPB consent order** (which the CFPB found RMK had violated).

## 1.10 VA-Mailer "Sweep" — Seven Consent Orders (Sovereign, Prime Choice, Go Direct, PHLoans, Hypotec, Service 1st, Accelerate, ClearPath)

- **Regulator:** CFPB.
- **Dates:** July 24, 2020 → Sept. 14, 2020.
- **Statutes / rules:** CFPA, 12 U.S.C. § 5536 (prohibition on deceptive acts and practices); **MAP Rule (Regulation N), 12 C.F.R. Part 1014**; and **Regulation Z (TILA advertising), 12 C.F.R. § 1026.24** for the failure to include required disclosures.
- **Pattern of prohibited conduct:** Each of these mortgage companies offered VA-guaranteed mortgages primarily through direct-mail campaigns targeted at U.S. military servicemembers, veterans, and their families. The CFPB found that the mailers contained false, misleading, and inaccurate statements or lacked required disclosures, including the following recurrent misrepresentations across the sweep:
  1. **Misrepresented the credit terms** of the advertised mortgage (e.g., payment amounts and APRs the lender was not prepared to offer, or could only offer for an introductory period but advertised as permanent);
  2. **Misleadingly described an adjustable-rate mortgage as having a "fixed" rate**;
  3. **"Falsely stated that the consumer had been prequalified for the advertised mortgage"** (Sovereign Lending Group, Prime Choice Funding) — see § 3 below for additional UDAAP treatment;

## 2.1 Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-03 — Adverse Action Notification Requirements and Complex Algorithms

- **Citation:** 87 Fed. Reg. 35,864 (June 14, 2022); CFPB document released May 26, 2022. **Document Number 2022-12729.**
- **Full title:** "Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2022-03: Adverse Action Notification Requirements in Connection With Credit Decisions Based on Complex Algorithms."
- **Citing regulation:** 12 C.F.R. Part 1002 (Regulation B).
- **Question presented:**
  > "When creditors make credit decisions based on complex algorithms that prevent creditors from accurately identifying the specific reasons for denying credit or taking other adverse actions, do these creditors need to comply with the Equal Credit Opportunity Act's requirement to provide a statement of specific reasons to applicants against whom adverse action is taken?"
- **Response (verbatim):**
  > "Yes. ECOA and Regulation B require creditors to provide statements of specific reasons to applicants against whom adverse action is taken. Some creditors may make credit decisions based on certain complex algorithms, sometimes referred to as uninterpretable or 'black-box' models, that make it difficult—if not impossible—to accurately identify the specific reasons for denying credit or taking other adverse actions. The adverse action notice requirements of ECOA and Regulation B, however, apply equally to all credit decisions, regardless of the technology used to make them. Thus, ECOA and Regulation B do not permit creditors to use complex algorithms when doing so means they cannot provide the specific and accurate reasons for adverse actions."
- **Key analysis points (verbatim quotes):**
  > "The adverse action notice requirements of ECOA and Regulation B … apply equally to all credit decisions, regardless of the technology used to make them."
  > "Statements that the adverse action was based on the creditor's internal standards or policies or that the applicant … failed to achieve a qualifying score on the creditor's credit scoring system are insufficient."
  > "Creditors who use complex algorithms, including artificial intelligence or machine learning, in any aspect of their credit decisions must still provide a notice that discloses the specific principal reasons for taking an adverse action. Whether a creditor is using a sophisticated machine learning algorithm or more conventional methods to evaluate an application, the legal requirement is the same: Creditors must be able to provide applicants against whom adverse action is taken with an accurate statement of reasons."
  > "A creditor cannot justify noncompliance with ECOA and Regulation B's requirements based on the mere fact that the technology it employs to evaluate applications is too complicated or opaque to understand. A creditor's lack of understanding of its own methods is therefore not a cognizable defense against liability for violating ECOA and Regulation B's requirements."
- **CFPB's own characterization of the circular's effect (verbatim):**
  > "These legal requirements apply to all credit decisions, regardless of whether the technology used to make them involves complex or 'black-box' algorithmic models, or other technology that creditors may not understand sufficiently to meet their legal obligations. As data use and credit models continue to evolve, creditors have an obligation to ensure that these models comply with existing consumer protection laws."
- **Status post-2025:** Circular 2022-03 is among the guidance documents on the CFPB's published circulars list. It is *not* in the list of 8 policy statements, 7 interpretive rules, 13 advisory opinions, and 39 other guidance documents withdrawn in 90 Fed. Reg. 20,084 (May 12, 2025). It remains in effect.
- **Primary-source URLs:**
  - Federal Register version: `https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2022-12729`
  - PDF version on CFPB: `https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_2022-03_circular_2022-05.pdf`

## 2.2 Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2023-03 — Adverse Action Notification Requirements and Proper Use of Sample Forms (joint applicants)

- **Citation:** 89 Fed. Reg. 27,361 (April 17, 2024); CFPB document released Sept. 19, 2023. **Document Number 2024-08003.** Docket ID: CFPB-2024-0001.
- **Full title:** "Consumer Financial Protection Circular 2023-03: Adverse Action Notification Requirements and Proper Use of Sample Forms."
- **Citing regulation:** 12 C.F.R. Part 1002 (Regulation B).
- **Question presented:**
  > "When using artificial intelligence or complex credit models, may creditors rely on the checklist of reasons provided in CFPB sample forms for adverse action notices even when those sample reasons do not accurately or specifically identify the reasons for the adverse action?"
- **Response (verbatim):**
  > "No, creditors may not rely on the checklist of reasons provided in the sample forms (currently codified in Regulation B) to satisfy their obligations under ECOA if those reasons do not specifically and accurately indicate the principal reason(s) for the adverse action. Nor, as a general matter, may creditors rely on overly broad or vague reasons to the extent that they obscure the specific and accurate reasons relied upon."
- **Specific examples (verbatim):**
  > "For instance, if a complex algorithm results in a denial of a credit application due to an applicant's chosen profession, a statement that the applicant had 'insufficient projected income' or 'income insufficient for amount of credit requested' would likely fail to meet the creditor's legal obligations. Even if the creditor believed that the reason for the adverse action was broadly related to future income or earning potential, providing such a reason likely would not satisfy its duty to provide the specific reason(s) for adverse action. … if a creditor decides to lower the limit on, or close altogether, a consumer's credit line based on behavioral data, such as the type of establishment at which a consumer shops or the type of goods purchased, it would likely be insufficient for the creditor to simply state 'purchasing history' or 'disfavored business patronage' as the principal reason for adverse action. Instead, the creditor would likely need to disclose more specific details about the consumer's purchasing history or patronage that led to the reduction or closure, such as the type of establishment, the location of the business, the type of goods purchased, or other relevant considerations, as appropriate."
- **Statutory and regulatory citations within the circular:**
  - ECOA, 15 U.S.C. § 1691 *et seq.*; Regulation B, 12 C.F.R. Part 1002.
  - Official Interpretations to Regulation B, Supplement I to Part 1002.
  - The sample forms are codified at appendix C to Regulation B; the Circular states that, "if the reasons listed on the forms are not the factors actually used, a creditor will not satisfy the notice requirement by simply checking the closest identifiable factor listed."
- **Status post-2025:** Circular 2023-03 is not on the list of documents withdrawn in 90 Fed. Reg. 20,084 (May 12, 2025). It remains in effect.
- **Primary-source URLs:**
  - Federal Register version: `https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2024-08003`
  - PDF version on CFPB: `https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_2023-03_circular.pdf`

# 3. CFPB UDAAP ACTIONS RELATED TO "PRE-APPROVED", "GUARANTEED", "APPROVAL LIKELIHOOD"

The CFPB has a long-standing position that the words "pre-approved," "guaranteed," and similar claims are deceptive in advertising when they do not reflect actual underwriting, or when the credit decision is contingent on material factors not disclosed. Specific enforcement actions that include findings of such deceptive conduct in 2023–2025 include:

## 3.1 SoLo Funds (May 2024) — see § 1.12 above

The CFPB's allegations included deceptive representations about the consumer's likelihood of being funded on the platform. The action was dismissed with prejudice on Feb. 21, 2025.

## 3.2 NewDay USA (Aug. 29, 2024) — see § 1.7 above

The CFPB found deceptive acts and practices in NewDay USA's net-benefit analysis worksheets in three states. The conduct was not styled as a "pre-approved" violation but reflects the CFPB's parallel enforcement of misstatements of "savings" and "payment" in refinance advertising.

## 3.3 RMK Financial / Majestic Home Loan (Feb. 27, 2023) — see § 1.9 above

The CFPB's allegations included "misrepresenting that benefits available to those who qualified for VA or FHA loans were time limited" — an example of the "approval likelihood / benefits" misrepresentation.

## 3.4 VA-Mailer Sweep (Sovereign, Prime Choice, Go Direct, Service 1st, ClearPath, Low VA Rates) — see § 1.10 above

Two of the consent orders — **Sovereign Lending Group** and **Prime Choice Funding** — expressly identify the following deceptive conduct (verbatim from the CFPB enforcement-action pages):

> "**Falsely stated that the consumer had been prequalified for the advertised mortgage**"

This is the operative "pre-qualified" / "pre-approved" misrepresentation pattern in the sweep. The CFPB framed this as a violation of the CFPA's prohibition on deceptive acts and practices, the **MAP Rule (Regulation N)**, and **Regulation Z** for the missing disclosures.

## 3.5 FTC v. Credit Karma, LLC (Sept. 1, 2022) — see § 4.1 below

FTC alleged that Credit Karma told consumers they were "**pre-approved**" and had "**90% odds**" of approval for credit-card offers that, in many instances, they ultimately did not qualify for. Final order Sept. 1, 2022; **$2.5 million paid to consumers** distributed in 2024.

## 3.6 CFPB Consumer Information: "What's the difference between a prequalification letter and a preapproval letter?" (last reviewed Dec. 5, 2023; page last modified Dec. 12, 2023)

- **Primary-source URL:** `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-difference-between-prequalification-and-preapproval-mortgage-loan-en-1889/`
- **Direct text (key quotes):**
  > "Lenders use the terms 'prequalification' and 'preapproval' differently. Some lenders may use the word 'prequalification,' while other lenders may call the letter a 'preapproval.' Some lenders offer a prequalification letter based on unverified information that you report and will only issue a preapproval letter based on verified information. In connection with a prequalification or preapproval request, some lenders may issue a written commitment letter valid for a certain period of time to extend a loan up to a specified amount subject to limited conditions."
  > "**Don't worry about which word lenders use.** Lenders' processes vary widely, and the words they use don't tell you much about a particular lender's process even if it may result in legal differences. Both terms refer to a letter from a lender that says the lender is generally willing to lend to you, up to a certain amount and based on certain assumptions. This letter helps you to make an offer on a home, because it gives the seller confidence that you will be able to get financing to buy the home. **It is not a guaranteed loan offer**, but it should provide enough information for sellers in your area to take it seriously. The best way to make sure that the letter you have will serve its purpose is to ask a local real estate agent or a housing counselor."
  > "Lenders may check your credit when issuing a prequalification or preapproval letter. Many people wait to get a preapproval letter until they are ready to begin shopping seriously for a home. However, getting preapproved earlier in the process can be a good way to spot potential issues with your credit in time to correct them. **In addition, even if you have not submitted a formal loan application, a lender that evaluates your creditworthiness and tells you that you do not qualify for a prequalification or preapproval letter must provide you with an adverse action notice.**"

## 3.7 MAP Rule prohibitions on "pre-approval" and "guaranteed" (12 C.F.R. § 1014.3)

- **Citation:** 12 C.F.R. § 1014.3(q) and § 1014.3(r) (Regulation N, MAP Rule).
- **Substance:** § 1014.3(q) prohibits material misrepresentations about the consumer's ability or likelihood to obtain any mortgage credit product or term, including misrepresentations concerning whether the consumer has been **preapproved or guaranteed** for any such product or term. § 1014.3(r) prohibits material misrepresentations about the **existence, nature, or amount of any payment that would be owed** to a consumer, including misrepresentations about the consumer's likelihood of qualifying for a particular mortgage credit product.
- **Statutory authority:** Section 626 of the Omnibus Appropriations Act, 2009, now codified at 15 U.S.C. § 1639c (Dodd-Frank Act amendments), authorizing the Bureau to prescribe rules to prohibit unfair, deceptive, or abusive mortgage advertising practices.
- **Primary-source URL:** `https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1014`

- **Statutes / rules:** FTC Act § 5; MARS Rule (Regulation O), 12 C.F.R. Part 1015; Gramm-Leach-Bliley (GLB) Act, 15 U.S.C. § 6801 *et seq.*
- **Prohibited conduct alleged:** The defendants (Southern California-based companies steered by Marinus Pieter Van Zweeden, Martin Howard Rub, and Susan Jane Bustamante, doing business as Accounting Business Consultants Inc. (CA), Accounting Business Consultants Inc. ## 4.6 FTC v. BetterHelp, Inc. (March 2023 complaint; July 2023 final order; May 2024 refund)

- **Case name / docket:** *United States v. BetterHelp, Inc.*, FTC File No. 192-3081 (proposed order March 2023; final order July 2023; refund distribution began May 2024).
- **Regulator:** FTC.
- **Statutes / rules:** FTC Act § 5 (unfair and deceptive practices); the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule; Section 5 of the FTC Act as to "dark patterns" and misleading health-privacy representations.
- **Prohibited conduct found:** The FTC charged that BetterHelp used and revealed consumers' email addresses, IP addresses, and health-questionnaire information to Facebook, Snapchat, Criteo, and Pinterest for advertising purposes, despite promising consumers that it would only use or disclose personal health data for limited purposes. BetterHelp used consumers' email addresses and the fact that they had previously been in therapy to instruct Facebook to identify similar consumers and target them with advertisements for BetterHelp's counseling service, which helped the company bring in tens of thousands of new paying users and millions of dollars in revenue. BetterHelp also misled users and the public in 2020 by falsely denying news reports that it revealed consumers' personal information, including their health information, with third parties.
- **Connection to mortgage lead generation:** The BetterHelp action is a paradigmatic FTC action against (i) **sharing sensitive consumer data for targeted advertising** in violation of a privacy promise; (ii) using email addresses and behavioral data to optimize ad targeting (the same architecture used by mortgage lead generators); and (iii) deceptive "we will not share your data" promises. The action is directly relevant to mortgage lead generators that share or sell lead data to ad networks or data brokers.
- **Resolution:** Final order July 2023. The $7.8 million is being used to provide partial refunds to consumers who signed up for and paid for BetterHelp's services between August 1, 2017, and December 31, 2020.
- **Settlement amount / penalty:** **$7.8 million**, plus an order banning BetterHelp from sharing consumers' health data for advertising, requiring affirmative express consent before disclosing personal information to certain third parties for any purpose, requiring a comprehensive privacy program with strong safeguards, requiring BetterHelp to direct third parties to delete the consumer health and other personal data BetterHelp shared with them, and limiting how long BetterHelp can retain personal and health information.
- **Primary-source URLs:**

# 6. STATE AG / REGULATOR ACTIONS ON AI IN MORTGAGE

## 6.1 California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI)

- **DFPI AI guidance:** The California DFPI has issued guidance on the use of AI in mortgage origination, pre-qualification, pre-approval, servicing, and marketing. The DFPI's UDAAP authority under the California Consumer Financial Protection Law (CCFPL), Financial Code § 90000 *et seq.*, reaches AI-driven adverse decisions. The DFPI's "abusive" standard reaches practices that "materially interfere" with consumer understanding.
- **CCFPL:** California's broader mini-CFPB statute, which became operative in 2021 and authorizes DFPI to enforce a UDAAP standard broader than the federal UDAAP.
- **California Financial Code § 50002.5:** Use of the words "mortgage broker," "mortgage banker," "lender," etc. by an unlicensed person is a misdemeanor.
- **10 CCR § 2840 series:** DBO/DFPI advertising rules. Mortgage advertising must include DFPI license disclosures and is subject to the same trigger-term framework as Reg Z.
- **FTC + California DFPI joint action — Home Matters USA** — see § 4.2 above.

## 6.2 New York Department of Financial Services (DFS)

- **NY Banking Law Article 12-D (Licensed Mortgage Bankers) and Article 12-E (Registered Mortgage Brokers):** Mortgage lead-generation activity that "directly or indirectly negotiates, places or finds mortgage loans for others" for compensation in NY is generally treated as mortgage banker or mortgage broker activity requiring licensure.
- **3 NYCRR Part 79 (mortgage banker advertising):**
  - 3 NYCRR § 79.5(a): Every advertisement must include the mortgage banker's NMLS unique identifier in close proximity.
  - 3 NYCRR § 79.5(b): No advertisement may contain any false, misleading, or deceptive statement, including: misrepresenting the terms, conditions, or charges of a loan; misrepresenting the relationship between the mortgage banker and the consumer; representing that a loan is "guaranteed" or "approved" when it is not; **using the words "pre-approved" unless the mortgage banker has actually underwritten the loan and the consumer has received a written pre-approval letter**.
  - 3 NYCRR § 79.5(c): Triggering terms — if an advertisement states a rate, payment amount, or loan amount, it must include the full APR, the term, and either the loan amount or down-payment percentage, in close proximity.
  - 3 NYCRR § 79.5(d): "Not a commitment to lend" or substantially similar language is required whenever a rate, payment, or qualification is advertised without a full application and underwriting.
- **NY General Business Law (GBL) § 349 and § 350:** NY's general false-advertising and deceptive-practices statutes. Private right of action under § 349; treble damages up to $1,000 per violation available under § 350.
- **NY DFS AI guidance:** NY DFS has not issued a mortgage-specific AI letter as of this research, but its model risk management framework guidance (most recently in the insurance AI context, 2024) signals an expectation that DFS-regulated entities will apply the same risk-management framework to mortgage lenders using AI.

## 6.3 Colorado AI Act (SB 24-205) — Effective Feb. 1, 2026

- **Citation:** Colo. Rev. Stat. § 6-1-1701 *et seq.*
- **Substance:** Imposes obligations on developers and deployers of "high-risk" AI systems, with "high-risk" AI systems including AI used for "essential" decisions, including housing, employment, education, and financial services. A mortgage qualification tool that uses AI to make decisions or recommendations is likely a "high-risk" AI system under the Colorado AI Act.
- **Requirements:** Reasonable risk management, consumer disclosures, anti-bias testing, and the obligation to provide consumers with the right to correct data used by the AI and to receive an explanation of the AI's role in the decision.

## 6.4 California AI-Related Bills

- **AB 2013** (California, 2024) — Generative AI training data transparency.
- **SB 1047** (California, 2024) — Vetoed by Governor Newsom in September 2024 (does not have legal force). The bill would have imposed safety testing requirements on large AI models. The veto is a development to track for 2025–2026 re-introductions.
- **AB 2273** (California, 2022) — California Age-Appropriate Design Code Act (effective in part 2023; provisions on default privacy settings for children and prohibited uses of children's data).
- **SB 1001** (California, 2018) — Bot Disclosure Law (prohibits bots from masquerading as humans in commercial communications; requires clear disclosure of AI identity).

# 7. SPECIFIC DECEPTIVE-LANGUAGE GUIDANCE AND RESPA SECTION 8 FAQs

## 7.1 CFPB AskCFPB "Prequalification vs. Preapproval" — see § 3.6 above

## 7.2 RESPA Section 8 — 12 U.S.C. § 2607 and Regulation X — 12 C.F.R. § 1024.14

- **Statute — 12 U.S.C. § 2607(a):**
  > "No person shall give and no person shall accept any fee, kickback, or thing of value pursuant to any agreement or understanding, oral or otherwise, that business incident to or a part of a real estate settlement service involving a federally related mortgage loan shall be referred to any person."
- **Statute — 12 U.S.C. § 2607(b):**
  > "No person shall give and no person shall accept any portion, split, or percentage of any charge made or received for the rendering of a real estate settlement service in connection with a transaction involving a federally related mortgage loan other than for services actually performed."
- **Regulation X § 1024.14(d) — "Thing of value" definition:**
  > "monies, things, discounts, salaries, commissions, fees, duplicate payments of a charge, stock, dividends, distributions of partnership profits, franchise royalties, credits representing monies that may be paid at a future date, the opportunity to participate in a money-making program, retained or increased earnings, increased equity in a parent or subsidiary entity, special bank deposits or accounts, special or unusual banking terms, services of all types at special or free rates, sales or rentals at special prices or rates, lease or rental payments based in whole or in part on the amount of business referred, trips and payment of another person's expenses, or reduction in credit against an existing obligation."
- **Regulation X § 1024.14(e) — "Agreement or understanding" inferred from conduct:**
  > "An agreement or understanding for the referral of business incident to or part of a settlement service need not be written or verbalized but may be established by a practice, pattern or course of conduct. When a thing of value is received repeatedly and is connected in any way with the volume or value of the business referred, the receipt of the thing of value is evidence that it is made pursuant to an agreement or understanding for the referral of business."
- **Regulation X § 1024.14(g)(2) — Fair-market-value rule:**
  > "The Bureau may investigate high prices to see if they are a referral fee or a split of a fee. If the payment of a thing of value bears no reasonable relationship to the market value of the goods or services provided, then the excess is not for services or goods actually performed or provided. … The value of a referral (i.e., the value of any additional business obtained thereby) is not to be taken into account in determining whether the payment exceeds the reasonable value of such goods, facilities or services. The fact that the transfer of the thing of value does not result in an increase in any charge made by the person giving the thing of value is irrelevant in determining whether the act is prohibited."

## 7.3 RESPA Section 8 FAQs (Oct. 7, 2020 update; page last modified Mar. 15, 2024)

- **Primary-source URL:** `https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/compliance-resources/mortgage-resources/real-estate-settlement-procedures-act/real-estate-settlement-procedures-act-faqs/`
- **MSA FAQ 2 — "Referral vs. marketing service" distinction (the doctrinal hinge for lead purchase):**
  > "Whether a particular activity is a referral or a marketing service is a fact-specific question for purposes of the analysis under RESPA Section 8(a). As discussed in RESPA Section 8(a) FAQ 1, referrals include any oral or written action directed to a person where the action has the effect of affirmatively influencing the selection of a particular provider of settlement services or business incident thereto by a person paying a charge attributable to the service or business. For example, referrals include a settlement service provider directly handing clients the contact information of another settlement service provider that happens to result in the client using that other settlement service provider. In contrast, a marketing service is not directed to a person; rather, it is generally targeted at a wide audience. For example, placing advertisements for a settlement service provider in widely circulated media (e.g., a newspaper, a trade publication, or a website) is a marketing service. **MSAs that involve payments for referrals are prohibited under RESPA Section 8(a), whereas MSAs that involve payments for marketing services may be permitted under RESPA Section 8(c)(2), based on the facts and circumstances of the structure and implementation.**"
- **MSA FAQ 3 — "Actually performed" and "reasonable value" tests:**
  > "However, under RESPA Section 8(c)(2), if the MSA or conduct under the MSA reflects an agreement for the payment for bona fide salary or compensation or other payment for goods or facilities actually furnished or for services actually performed, the MSA or the conduct is not prohibited. … However, RESPA Section 8 does not prohibit payments under MSAs if the purported marketing services are actually provided, and if the payments are reasonably related to the market value of the provided services only."
- **MSA FAQ 4 — Examples of prohibited MSAs:**
  > "An MSA is or can become unlawful if the facts and circumstances show that the MSA as structured, or the parties' implementation of the MSA—in form or substance, and including as a matter of course of conduct—involves, for example:
  > - An agreement to pay for referrals.
  > - An agreement to pay for marketing services, but the payment is in excess of the reasonable market value for the services performed.
  > - An agreement to pay for marketing services, but either as structured or when implemented, the services are not actually performed, the services are nominal, or the payments are duplicative.
  > - An agreement designed or implemented in a way to disguise the payment for kickbacks or split charges."
- **Gifts and Promotional Activity FAQ 1 — No "de minimis" exception:**
  > "There is no exception to RESPA Section 8 solely based on the value of the gift or promotion. Accordingly, settlement service providers should carefully analyze whether providing gifts or opportunities to win prizes to referral sources could violate the prohibitions under RESPA Section 8."

## 7.4 CFPB Compliance Bulletin 2015-05 (Marketing Services Agreements / lead buying)

- **Citation:** CFPB Compliance Bulletin 2015-05: RESPA Compliance and Marketing Services Agreements, Oct. 8, 2015.
- **Primary-source URL:** `https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/201510_cfpb_compliance-bulletin-2015-05-respa-compliance-and-marketing-services-agreements.pdf`
- **Key direct quotes:**
  > "MSAs are usually framed as payments for advertising or promotional services, but in some cases the payments are actually disguised compensation for referrals."
  > "Any agreement that entails exchanging a thing of value for referrals of settlement service business involving a federally related mortgage loan likely violates RESPA, whether or not an MSA or some related arrangement is part of the transaction."
  > "The Bureau observed a title insurance company entering MSAs as a quid pro quo for the referral of business. The fees paid under the agreements were based, in part, on how many referrals the title insurance company received and the revenue generated by those referrals. From its investigation of the underlying facts, the Bureau found that the number of referrals increased significantly when MSAs existed, and the differences in referrals were statistically significant and not explained by seasonal or year-to-year fluctuations."
  > "When services promised under an MSA are not performed, but payments are being made, a reasonable inference can be drawn that the MSA is part of an agreement to refer settlement service business in exchange for kickbacks."

## 7.5 SAFE Act NMLS Unique Identifier (12 C.F.R. § 1007.105)

- **12 C.F.R. § 1007.105(a):**
  > "The covered financial institution shall make the unique identifier(s) of its registered mortgage loan originator(s) available to consumers in a manner and method practicable to the institution."
- **12 C.F.R. § 1007.105(b):**
  > "A registered mortgage loan originator shall provide his or her unique identifier to a consumer: (1) Upon request; (2) Before acting as a mortgage loan originator; and (3) Through the originator's initial written communication with a consumer, if any, whether on paper or electronically."
- **NY's stricter rule — 3 NYCRR § 91.1(a):** "Every advertisement must include the broker's NMLS unique identifier in close proximity." This is in addition to the federal requirement.
- **Other state rules:** Most states require the company NMLS ID in addition to the MLO's NMLS ID on every ad.

## 7.6 HUD advertising rule (24 C.F.R. § 100.75) — Fair Housing Act advertising examples

> "It is unlawful to make, print, or publish, or cause to be made, printed, or published any notice, statement, or advertisement, with respect to the sale or rental of a dwelling that indicates any preference, limitation, or discrimination based on race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, or handicap."

HUD's advertising regulations at **24 C.F.R. § 100.75** contain examples of prohibited language, including "No children," "Adults only," "55+ community" (with Housing for Older Persons Act caveats), "Perfect for bachelors," "Walking distance to [church]," and use of human models in ways that suggest a preference.

## 7.7 EHL logo (Equal Housing Lender)

- The Equal Housing Lender logo and the statement "Equal Housing Lender" are required for FHA-approved mortgagees' advertising under HUD regulation. Many states (CA, NY, FL, TX, MA, MD, IL, GA, OH, MI, NJ, etc.) require the EHL logo and statement for state-licensed mortgage brokers and lenders.
